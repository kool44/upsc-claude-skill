# Personality Test (Interview)

> Data-point convention: any figure marked **[UPDATE]** must be checked against the latest UPSC notification, e-summon letter or official source before being quoted to a learner. Nothing in this file is dated after 2024.

## What it is and how it is scored

**Place in the exam.** The Personality Test is the final stage of the Civil Services Examination. It carries **275 marks**; the Mains written papers (Essay + GS I-IV + Optional I-II, 250 each) carry **1750**, so final merit is out of **2025**. The qualifying papers (Paper A Indian language, Paper B English) do not count towards merit. UPSC prescribes no minimum qualifying mark in the interview; final rank is the sum of written and interview marks. **[UPDATE: confirm on upsc.gov.in each year.]**

**Format.** One board, typically **five members**: a chairperson who is a UPSC Member, plus four other members (retired civil servants, academics, domain experts, armed forces officers and similar). The candidate sits across a table; the chairperson usually opens from the DAF, then passes to each member in turn, and closes. Duration is typically **20-35 minutes**; a shorter or longer interview is not a reliable signal of marks. Boards are allotted on the day; candidates do not choose them. Forenoon-session candidates typically report around 9 a.m. and afternoon-session candidates around 1 p.m. (the e-summon letter states the exact time); expect to spend the better part of half a day at Dholpur House (UPSC, Shahjahan Road, New Delhi).

**What is assessed (UPSC notification wording).** The notification states that the object of the interview is "to assess the personal suitability of the candidate for a career in public service by a Board of competent and unbiased observers", that the test "is intended to judge the mental calibre of a candidate", and that it "is not intended to be a test either of the specialised or general knowledge of the candidates which has been already tested through their written papers". The qualities named are:

- **mental alertness**
- **critical powers of assimilation**
- **clear and logical exposition**
- **balance of judgement**
- **variety and depth of interest**
- **ability for social cohesion and leadership**
- **intellectual and moral integrity**

The notification also describes the technique as "not that of a strict cross-examination but of a natural, though directed and purposive conversation", and says candidates are expected to have "taken an intelligent interest not only in their special subjects of academic study but also in the events which are happening around them both within and outside their own State or country as well as in modern currents of thought and in new discoveries which should rouse the curiosity of well educated youth". Treat each of the seven qualities as a rubric line; every answer should visibly demonstrate at least one.

**Score ranges (qualitative only).** Marks are not published as a distribution; from candidates' disclosed marksheets, most cluster in a broad middle band, with smaller tails clearly above and below. Do not quote fixed numbers to learners (they vary by year and board). What matters for strategy: the gap between a weak and a strong interview is large enough to reorder many ranks, and it is earned in half an hour rather than over two years of written preparation.

**Why it moves rank, service and cadre.** Service allocation (IAS, IFS, IPS, IRS and the rest) and cadre allocation follow rank, category and stated preference, so the interview routinely decides whether a candidate gets a first-preference service and a preferred zone or cadre. This is also why candidates with modest Mains totals can still finish high, and vice versa.

- **Medical examination.** It is a fitness classification under the prescribed medical standards, not an additional ranking filter; a candidate may be declared fit, fit with a condition, or temporarily unfit and re-examined. Disclose conditions honestly, carry spectacles and prescriptions. Non-attendance can hold up allocation.
- **Document verification.** Carry originals and self-attested copies of everything the e-summon letter lists and everything claimed in the DAF: matriculation certificate (date of birth), degree and marksheets, category/EWS/PwBD certificates in the prescribed format and validity period, employment undertaking or NOC where applicable, photo ID, photographs, and certificates for any prize or position claimed. Discrepancies between DAF claims and documents are a red flag for integrity; sort them out before the day.

**DAF-I vs DAF-II.** DAF-I is filled at the Mains application stage (personal details, education, employment, optional subject, medium, hobbies, achievements). DAF-II, filled after the Mains result, typically adds **service preferences**, **zone and cadre preferences** (under the zonal cadre allocation policy, candidates rank zones and then cadres within each zone) and other details needed for allocation. Both are in front of the board, and both are examined for consistency.

## The DAF is the syllabus

The board has the DAF in front of it; it does not have your written marks. Candidates consistently report that a large share of questions originates in the DAF. Go through it field by field and build a written question bank; your spoken answers must agree with the paper in front of them.

### Name and its meaning
Likely lines:
1. What does your name mean? Who named you and why?
2. Is there a historical, mythological or literary figure with your name? What do you know about them?
3. Your surname indicates a community or region: tell us about it.
4. Do you share a name with a well-known person? What do you think of them?
5. If a name connotes caste or region, boards may ask neutral questions on that region's history or a reform movement there.
Prepare: etymology, namesake figures (one paragraph each), the region or community behind the surname (history, a reformer, a festival), and a composed one-line answer if the name has no special meaning ("My parents liked it; it means X in Sanskrit").

### Home state, district, town
Likely lines:
1. Tell us about your district: economy, crops, industries, rivers, famous people.
2. What is the biggest problem your district faces and what would you do as its DM?
3. Why is your state ahead or behind on literacy, sex ratio, per capita income, agrarian distress, migration?
4. A current controversy or project in the state (dam, mine, industrial corridor, language, reservation, statehood demand).
5. One cultural asset from your state (dance form, craft with a GI tag, monument, festival) and its significance.
6. Your state's governor, CM and notable schemes; the state's relationship with the Centre (fiscal devolution, river water).
7. If you have lived elsewhere for study or work, compare the two places.
Prepare: a two-page dossier on district and state (see Preparation plan, week 2), and three "if I were DM" plans for the top local problems.

### Education (school, college, subjects, final-year project)
Likely lines:
1. Why did you choose this course or college? What did you learn there beyond the syllabus?
2. Explain your final-year project to a lay person. What problem did it solve? What would you do differently?
3. Conceptual questions from the degree: an engineer may be asked about a bridge failure, renewable energy or semiconductors; a doctor about public health; a commerce graduate about GST or bank NPAs; a humanities graduate about a thinker or theory.
4. Why is your academic score low or high? Gap years?
5. How is your subject relevant to administration?
6. Latest developments in your field (last 2-3 years).
Prepare: a 90-second project explanation at three levels (lay person, graduate, examiner); five core concepts you can draw on paper; three field-to-governance links.

### Work experience (company, role, industry)
Likely lines:
1. What exactly did you do? Describe a typical day.
2. What did your company make or sell, who were its customers, what is its business model?
3. Why leave? What did you dislike? (Never run down the employer.)
4. What did you learn that a civil servant needs?
5. Your industry's regulatory environment and recent policy issues (data protection law for IT, labour codes for manufacturing, RBI norms for fintech).
6. Have you managed people? Handled a crisis? Been overruled?
7. If employed now: have you informed your employer? Do you have an NOC or the required undertaking?
Prepare: a company fact sheet (founding, scale, sector, competitors, recent news), your role in STAR format (situation, task, action, result) for 3-4 episodes, and the sector's policy map.

### Hobbies and interests
Likely lines:
1. "Reading" -> last book, favourite author, a book that changed your view, a line you remember.
2. "Cricket/football" -> rules, a recent tournament, governance of the sport (BCCI, the Lodha Committee reforms upheld by the Supreme Court in 2016), doping, national sports policy.
3. "Travelling" -> last trip, what you observed about governance there, tourism policy.
4. "Cooking" -> a dish, its chemistry, food safety regulation (FSSAI), nutrition programmes.
5. "Music/painting" -> a raga or style, an exponent, state patronage of the arts.
6. "Trekking/yoga/fitness" -> physiology, a specific trek, Himalayan ecology.
7. Any hobby -> "demonstrate", "name three practitioners", or "how will it help you as an officer".
Prepare: depth over breadth. For each listed hobby: history, 3-5 technical terms, 3 well-known practitioners, one recent news item, one link to public policy, one personal anecdote. If you cannot sustain ten minutes on a hobby, do not list it.

### Optional subject
Likely lines:
1. Why this optional? Is it your graduation subject? If not, why the change?
2. Big-idea questions: a sociologist on caste today; a geographer on urban floods; a historian on a disputed interpretation; an anthropologist on tribal policy; a public administration candidate on a reform commission.
3. How will the optional help you as an administrator?
4. A thinker or theory applied to a current issue.
Prepare: ten "big ideas" of the optional, each with a current application; the three thinkers most quoted in its Paper I; one honest sentence on why you chose it.

### Service preference and cadre preference
Likely lines:
1. Why IAS over IPS or IFS (or vice versa)? What if you get your third preference?
2. Why home cadre? Would you serve in the North-East?
3. What is the difference between IAS and IPS roles at the district level?
4. Why not IRS, given your commerce or finance background?
5. If IFS is low on your list: are you not interested in India's foreign policy?
6. If already in a service from an earlier attempt: why appear again?
Prepare: a role description for each of your top five services, a non-defensive reason for each ranking, and a sincere "I will serve where allotted" backed by actual knowledge of that service and zone.

### Prizes, positions, extra-curriculars, achievements
Likely lines:
1. What was the competition? Who else participated? What did you learn?
2. You were a class representative or club secretary: an example of leadership, a conflict resolved.
3. NCC/NSS/scouting: what did you do, what did you observe about rural India?
4. Olympiad or scholarship: do you still follow the subject?
5. A gap between a claimed achievement and depth of knowledge is tested quickly.
Prepare: one 60-second story per entry with a lesson at the end.

### Filling the DAF honestly and defensibly
- Write only what you can discuss for ten minutes. Every word is a potential question.
- Be specific but not grandiose: "reading Indian political biographies" is better than "reading"; "amateur long-distance running (two half-marathons)" is better than "fitness".
- Do not list a hobby you took up last month. Do not list "social service" without a concrete organisation and duration.
- Keep the employment section complete and chronological; unexplained gaps will be asked about; explain them honestly (preparation, health, family).
- Service and cadre preferences must be defensible, not strategic-looking. A technologist putting IFS first and IAS fourth will be asked why.
- Keep a PDF copy of the submitted DAF. Your question bank is built from it, and mock panels must be given the same document.
- Documents claimed must exist in the prescribed format before the DAF is submitted.

## Preparation plan (8-10 weeks after Mains)

Assumptions: working professional, ~2 h on weeknights, ~7 h each weekend day (about 24 h/week). A full-timer can compress weeks 1-5 into three weeks and spend the saved time on extra peer mocks and current-affairs depth, not on more coaching mocks. Start the day the Mains result is expected, not when it arrives; e-summon letters go out in batches and the first batch typically follows the result by a few weeks.

**Week 1: DAF audit and question bank.**
- Print the DAF. For every field write 8-15 questions (target **200+** by the end of the week). Group: name, place, education, work, hobbies, optional, services, achievements.
- Weeknights: write one-paragraph answers to 10 questions per night. Weekends: record yourself answering 20 of them aloud, 60-90 seconds each; cut filler words.
- Start a current-affairs log (see week 3) from today's newspaper.

**Week 2: Home state and district.**
- Build a dossier: geography (rivers, soils, climate, forests, disasters), economy (GSDP sector shares, major crops, industries, PSUs, ports, mines), demography (Census 2011 indicators **[UPDATE if a newer Census is published]**: literacy, sex ratio, SC/ST share, migration), administration (districts, divisions, panchayat structure, urban bodies), social issues (3-5 live ones), culture (dance, music, crafts with GI tags, cuisine, monuments, festivals), famous people (freedom fighters, scientists, writers, sportspersons), politics (CM, governor, recent elections, major state schemes), Centre-State friction points (river water, fiscal devolution, language).
- Sources: the state's latest Economic Survey (state finance or planning department website), the district website and the District Census Handbook (Census 2011), the state tourism portal, the India Year Book (Publications Division) chapter on states, and Wikipedia for a first map that you then verify.
- Output: three "if I were DM" plans and 10 crisp facts you can say without hesitation.

**Week 3: Current affairs of the last 12 months and opinion-forming.**
- Themes: national politics and polity (major Acts, Supreme Court rulings, elections, federal issues), economy (Union Budget, RBI policy stance, inflation, employment, trade, major reforms), international (India's neighbourhood, major conflicts, multilateral summits India hosted or attended, India-US/China/Russia/EU), science and technology (space missions, digital public infrastructure, AI policy, biotech, defence), environment (climate conferences, heatwaves and floods, forest and wildlife policy), social sector (health, education, nutrition, women, gender-related rulings), sports and culture (major wins, awards).
- Method: one page per theme with facts, two sides, your stand, and one line on what the administration should do. This "fact, two sides, stand, action" template is the opinion-forming drill.
- Sources: a daily newspaper (The Hindu or The Indian Express editorial and explainer pages), PIB for government positions, Yojana and Kurukshetra issues of the year, PRS Legislative Research for bills, and one monthly current-affairs compilation for coverage.
- Weeknights: 1 h newspaper + 1 h theme page. Weekends: 2 h revision, 2 h opinion drill, the rest for DAF practice.

**Week 4: Graduation subject refresh and work-experience linkage.**
- Re-read conceptual chapters of 2-3 core subjects (not derivations). Engineers: materials, energy, structures, electronics basics, networks, databases as applicable; doctors: public health, epidemiology; commerce: accounting basics, taxation, banking; humanities: three thinkers and three debates.
- The last 2-3 years of developments in the field (industry reports, journal news pages).
- Work: write the company fact sheet; write four STAR episodes (leadership, failure, ethics, teamwork); map five things you did to five things a DM, SP or Municipal Commissioner does (project management, stakeholder negotiation, data-based monitoring, procurement, grievance handling).

**Week 5: Optional big ideas, services and roles, situational questions.**
- Optional: ten big ideas with current applications; five likely "apply X to Y" questions.
- Services: role and career path of each service in your top five; district-level roles of DM/Collector, SP and DFO, and the Collector-versus-District Magistrate distinction; recent debates (lateral entry, Mission Karmayogi, police reforms since Prakash Singh (2006), generalist versus specialist).
- Situational: write 15 "as DM/SP, what would you do" scenarios (communal tension, flood, exam paper leak, illegal mining, political pressure on a transfer, a subordinate's corruption, a protest blocking a highway, child labour in a factory, hospital oxygen shortage, a cyber-fraud wave). Use a fixed frame: secure life and law, gather facts, invoke the rule or scheme, coordinate agencies, communicate, follow up, record.
- Ethics: refresh GS IV vocabulary (integrity, impartiality, objectivity, empathy, conflict of interest) and have one personal example for each.

**Weeks 6-7: Mock interviews.**
- Number: 3-6 formal mocks is enough; more can flatten you into a coaching template. Mix at least two different panels (coaching-institute boards, retired-officer panels, alumni associations, university placement cells, online panels). Add 4-6 peer mocks with other candidates using each other's DAFs.
- Use feedback this way: after each mock, write down every question asked, note which of the seven qualities the weak answers failed, rewrite three answers, and re-record them. Discard contradictory style advice; keep content corrections. Do not change your personality between mocks.
- Weeknights: one formal mock per week plus re-recording. Weekends: peer mocks and DAF question-bank revision.

**Week 8: Body language, dress, entry and exit, stress.**
- Dress: men typically a plain formal shirt, trousers, tie optional, a blazer in winter, polished shoes; women typically a saree, salwar-kameez or formal western wear in sober colours. Nothing new on the day; wear the outfit in a mock.
- Entry: knock or wait to be called, walk in, greet the chair and the board ("Good morning, sir/ma'am"), wait to be asked to sit, thank them, sit upright with hands on the lap or table, feet flat. Exit: thank the board, do not rush, push the chair back quietly.
- Eye contact with the questioner, brief glances at the others; nod to show listening; do not interrupt; keep hands still; smile when it is natural.
- Stress: slow nasal breathing before entry, a fixed pre-interview routine (sleep, meal, travel time with a buffer), a one-line reminder ("conversation, not examination"). Practise a short pause before answering difficult questions.
- Logistics: reach Delhi a day earlier if travelling; print the e-summon letter; verify documents against the DAF checklist.

**Weeks 9-10 (if the slot is late): revision and maintenance.**
- Weeknights: newspaper plus one DAF section recorded aloud. Weekends: one peer mock, revise current-affairs theme pages, update them with the week's news.
- Last week before the slot: no new mocks after T-3 days; re-read the DAF, the state dossier and the current-affairs pages; rehearse the 10 most likely opening questions ("Tell us about yourself" is reported less often than in corporate interviews, but prepare a 60-second version anyway); sleep.
- Interview day: light breakfast, arrive 30 minutes before reporting time, keep a newspaper for the waiting hall, talk little, do not compare notes with candidates coming out.

## Question types and how to answer

General rules: answer the question asked, in 30-90 seconds; lead with the direct answer, then one or two reasons, then stop; if you do not know, say so briefly; never argue, never flatter; keep facts to those you are sure of.

### Factual on DAF
Examples: "What is the population of your district?" "What was your final-year project?" "Name three rivers of your state."
Approach: exact, short, confident. If you are approximately sure, say "about 2 million as per the 2011 Census". If unsure, say "I am not certain, but I believe it is around X". Never guess a number you cannot defend; one wrong number invites a chain of cross-questions.

### Opinion on a contested issue
Examples: "Should India have a Uniform Civil Code?" "Is the freebie culture harming the economy?" "Should the collegium system be replaced?"
Approach: name the two sides in one sentence each, state your stand in one sentence, give the single strongest reason, and add the constitutional or institutional guardrail ("subject to Article 25 and the Law Commission's consultative process"). Balanced means you have weighed both; evasive means you have refused to choose. The board wants to see judgement, so choose, but hold it lightly: "I would revise this view if the data showed...". Do not personally attack any party, court or community.

### Situational/administrative (as DM/SP)
Examples: "You are DM; a rumour on social media triggers a mob outside a police station." "You are SP; a minister's son is involved in a hit-and-run." "You are DM; a flood has cut off three blocks and the CM is arriving tomorrow."
Approach (sequence, aloud): (1) immediate priority, usually life and law and order; (2) facts: verify, reach the site or send the SDM/DSP; (3) legal tools: Section 163 of the BNSS 2023 (formerly Section 144 CrPC) for prohibitory orders, the Disaster Management Act 2005, relevant BNS (formerly IPC) sections; (4) coordination: police, health, revenue, NDRF/SDRF, media cell; (5) communication: a public message to kill the rumour, a press briefing; (6) follow-up: FIR, relief, inquiry, report to the state. Say what you would do, not what "the government should do". Show that political pressure does not change the lawful course of action, without theatrics.

### Ethical dilemma
Examples: "Your senior asks you to sign a file you believe is irregular." "A contractor offers to fund the district's school for a tender favour." "You find your close friend's firm has violated environmental norms."
Approach: identify the values in tension (loyalty versus legality, development versus rule of law), state the non-negotiable (no illegal act, no personal gain), choose the least-harm lawful route (record dissent in writing, escalate through channel, accept the lawful benefit without the quid pro quo), and mention the cost you accept (transfer, friendship). Use GS IV vocabulary sparingly; one personal example from work beats three definitions.

### "Why civil services" / "Why leave your job" / "Why this service preference"
Examples: "You earn well in IT; why this?" "Why IAS and not IPS?" "What if you get IRS?"
Approach: one concrete motive rooted in your life (a specific problem you saw, a specific exposure), one honest acknowledgement of what you give up, one statement of what you bring. Avoid "I want to serve the nation" as the only line. For preferences: describe the role you want in operational terms ("district-level coordination of schemes and crisis response") and show that you know what the third preference does. "I will serve where I am allotted" is acceptable only when followed by evidence that you know that service.

### Hobby depth
Examples: "You read: which book is on your table now?" "You play chess: explain the Sicilian Defence." "You trek: what is acute mountain sickness?"
Approach: specific names, terms and one anecdote; then connect to the officer's life if asked ("trekking taught me logistics and patience"). If asked to sing or recite, make a short, composed attempt or decline politely once ("I would rather not, sir, but I can describe the raga"). Never bluff a technical term.

### Current-affairs linkage
Examples: "What does the latest RBI policy stance mean for a district economy?" "How does India hosting a multilateral summit matter to a farmer?" "What did the Supreme Court hold on electoral bonds (2024), Article 370 (2023) or same-sex marriage (2023)?"
Approach: fact (what happened), meaning (why it matters), link (to the DAF field or to administration). Keep to events from the last 12-18 months and to what you have actually read; do not reconstruct a judgment from memory of headlines.

### Hypothetical
Examples: "If you were Education Minister for a day, what one change?" "If there were no civil services, who would run districts?" "If you had Rs 100 crore for your district?"
Approach: pick one thing, justify it with a problem you know, and say how you would measure success. Avoid listing five things. A touch of imagination is welcome; a lecture is not.

### Stress questions
Examples: "Your marks are mediocre; why should we select you?" "You engineers only come here for status." "That answer was wrong, wasn't it?"
Approach: stay level, do not take the bait, do not apologise excessively. Acknowledge the premise where true, correct it where false, in one sentence each: "My academic marks were average; my work record since shows sustained performance." If told you are wrong and you are sure, hold your view politely with your reason; if not sure, say "I may be mistaken; I would like to check that". The board is testing composure, not the fact.

### "I don't know" handling
Approach: say it early and cleanly ("I am not aware of that, sir"), optionally add the nearest thing you do know ("I have read about the related X; I am not sure about Y"), and stop. Do not fill silence with guesses. Two or three honest "I don't know"s in an interview are normal; ten suggest thin preparation.

### Questions on your state's problems
Examples: "Why does your state report so many farmer suicides?" "Why is your state's sex ratio poor?" "Why do your state's youth migrate?"
Approach: causes in two layers (structural plus proximate), what the state and Centre are doing (name the scheme or Act), what you would add as an officer, with one number you are sure of. Do not become defensive about your state; do not run it down either.

### Questions on your name
Examples: "Your name is that of a deity; what is the story?" "Your surname suggests you are from community X; what is their traditional occupation?"
Approach: short, factual, unembarrassed; if the question strays into caste or religion, answer the factual part and steer to a neutral extension ("the community is known for X craft, which has a GI tag"). Do not volunteer political views on identity unless asked.

## Working-professional and engineer/IT-specific angles

Boards see many IT and engineering candidates; the differentiator is domain honesty and governance linkage, not jargon. Likely questions and credible approaches:

- **"Why leave a good job?"** Give a dated, specific trigger (a project in a government domain, a volunteering experience, a family circumstance) and what you have done since to test the motive (field exposure, courses, the attempts themselves). Mention the salary cut once, matter-of-factly, and move on.
- **"What will you bring from the private sector?"** Three transferable practices with examples: measurable targets and dashboards, process documentation, stakeholder management, deadline culture, vendor and contract management. Then one sentence of humility: "I will need to learn the law and the field; the sector does not teach those."
- **"Is the private sector more efficient than government?"** Balance: efficiency on narrow metrics versus equity, due process and scale; cite one public success (UPI and the digital payments stack, CoWIN-based vaccination logistics) and one private failure (regulatory arbitrage, the NPA build-up of the 2010s).
- **"Technology in governance."** Be concrete: Aadhaar-based DBT and its exclusion risks, UPI, CoWIN, GeM, PM GatiShakti, land-record digitisation (DILRMP), e-Courts. Say what technology cannot fix (last-mile trust, grievance redress) and what the officer's job is (process redesign, not just an app).
- **"Startups and job creation."** Know the Startup India framework (2016), the angel-tax debate (abolition announced in the Union Budget of July 2024 **[UPDATE for current status]**), unicorn concentration in a few cities, gig work and social security (Code on Social Security, 2020). Connect to your district: one startup-enabling step a DM can take (an incubator with a local college, single-window clearances).
- **"AI and jobs; should we regulate AI?"** Explain in lay terms what current AI does, the realistic effect on IT services and BPO, reskilling, and a measured regulatory stance (risk-based, sectoral, data protection first). Do not predict dates for artificial general intelligence or make sweeping claims.
- **"Data privacy."** Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 basics (consent, data fiduciary, Data Protection Board of India, exemptions for the State), the Puttaswamy judgment (2017) recognising privacy as a fundamental right, the tension between welfare targeting and surveillance, and your view on the exemptions.
- **"Your company's domain."** A banking-tech engineer gets asked about core banking, UPI and frauds; a telecom engineer about spectrum, 5G and BharatNet; an automotive engineer about EV policy and FAME; a semiconductor engineer about the India Semiconductor Mission; a SaaS engineer about data localisation and cross-border data flows. Prepare the sector's regulator, flagship policy and one live controversy.
- **"Will you be able to work with illiterate villagers / a political boss / a slow system?"** Give one episode where you worked with a non-technical client or a difficult manager, and the posture you took.
- **"What is wrong with the way government uses technology?"** Procurement cycles, vendor lock-in, poor data interoperability, pilots that never scale, the digital divide; and one fix you would push as an officer.
- **"Have you worked abroad or for a foreign firm? Why serve India then?"** Answer without nationalism as a crutch: what you saw of good systems there, and what you want to replicate.
- Avoid: calling government "inefficient" as a blanket statement; promising to "digitise everything"; treating the board as a client to be sold a product.

## Mock interview mode (how Claude runs one)

Protocol when the learner asks for a mock:

1. **Collect the DAF summary.** Ask for (or accept a pasted) summary: name and its meaning, home state and district, school, college, degree and project, employment (company, role, years), hobbies, achievements, optional, service and cadre preferences, any gaps or special circumstances. If the learner gives only a few fields, run with those and say which rounds will be thin.
2. **Set the frame.** State the variant, that you will play a five-member board (label questions "Chair", "M1".."M4"), that answers should be spoken-length (30-90 seconds, 60-150 words), and that you will not give feedback until the end unless the learner asks for a pause.
3. **Run 12-15 questions, one at a time.** Open from the DAF (name, place, education), then rotate: work, hobby, optional, state, current affairs, situational, ethics, service preference, one hypothetical, one stress question. Adapt like a board: follow up on a weak or interesting answer (2-3 follow-ups at most), probe a number the learner quoted, challenge an opinion with the other side once, and move on when an answer is complete. Mix easy and hard. Do not ask trick trivia.
4. **Feedback per answer**, after the last question, in a table keyed by question number with four columns: **content** (accuracy, specificity, missing point), **balance** (took a stand? acknowledged the other side?), **brevity** (word count, where to cut), **confidence markers in wording** (hedges like "I think maybe", filler, over-apology, absolutes like "always/never"; recommend replacements). Quote the learner's phrase when flagging it.
5. **Summary**: three strengths, three fixes (each with a concrete drill, e.g., "rewrite Q7 in 80 words with one number"), a qualitative band (well below / around / above the typical middle band; no numeric marks), and the five questions to rehearse next.
6. **Offer a rerun** of the three weakest questions immediately.

**Variants** (learner picks, or Claude proposes based on the weakest area):
- **DAF-only round**: all 12-15 questions from the DAF fields; stress on consistency and depth.
- **Current-affairs round**: 12 questions from the last 12 months across national, international, economy, S&T and environment, each tied back to the learner's state, sector or service.
- **Stress round**: a deliberately sceptical board; interruptions, challenges, "you are wrong"; feedback focuses on composure and language.
- **State round**: district and state geography, economy, issues, culture, famous people, schemes, with three "as DM" scenarios.
- **Service round**: role of each preferred service, cadre questions, "why not X".
- **Situational/ethics round**: 10 scenarios, each answered in the six-step frame.

Claude's conduct: stay in role during the questions; do not praise mid-mock; keep follow-ups natural; if the learner asks a factual question back, answer briefly after the mock, not during.

## Do and don't list

Drawn from candidates' commonly reported experiences; general, not attributed to any specific board.

- Do read your DAF the night before and the morning of the interview; most opening questions come from its first page.
- Do greet the whole board, wait to be asked to sit, and thank them at the end; small courtesies are noticed.
- Do answer the question asked; a precise "yes, because X" beats a two-minute tour.
- Do say "I don't know" cleanly and early; boards reward honesty and punish bluffing.
- Do carry one number per theme you are sure of; one accurate figure signals preparation more than five approximate ones.
- Do take a short pause before a hard question; a few seconds of silence reads as thought, not panic.
- Do keep opinions balanced but committed: state both sides, then choose.
- Do connect answers to your own experience when it is genuine; personal examples are remembered.
- Do dress plainly and comfortably; nothing you have not worn before.
- Do keep documents in the DAF's order in one folder; verification goes faster and calmer.
- Do treat the waiting hall as part of the test: stay calm, avoid comparing notes, read the newspaper.
- Don't contradict your DAF; any mismatch between paper and speech becomes an integrity question.
- Don't argue with a board member; hold a view politely once, then concede the possibility of error if unsure.
- Don't use coaching jargon ("holistic", "multi-pronged", "paradigm shift") as a substitute for a point.
- Don't criticise your employer, your state, a community, a court or a political party by name in personal terms.
- Don't quote figures or judgments you have not read; a probing follow-up will expose it.
- Don't over-apologise, over-smile or over-nod; one apology for a mistake is enough.
- Don't volunteer a hobby, book or achievement you cannot sustain for ten minutes.
- Don't read interview length or the board's demeanour as a signal of marks; stern boards give high marks and friendly ones give low ones often enough.
- Don't change your personality for the mocks' sake; a consistent, calm, honest candidate scores better than a rehearsed one.
