# Essay Paper

Read this when the learner asks about the Essay paper: its pattern, past topics, how to choose a topic, how to structure and practise, or when they submit an essay for grading. Prelims-style content maps live in the subject files; this file is about *writing*, not *knowing*.

## Pattern and what scores

**Shape of the paper (typically; verify the current year's notification and question paper on upsc.gov.in)**

- One paper, 250 marks, 3 hours. Counts fully toward the Mains merit total of 1750 (seven papers of 250 each), unlike the two qualifying language papers.
- Two sections, A and B, four topics each. Write **one essay from each section**: 125 marks each. The paper asks for "about 1000–1200 words" per essay.
- Medium: UPSC's rule is that all Mains papers other than the qualifying language papers must be written in the same medium, chosen in the application form (English or a language of the Eighth Schedule). The essay therefore takes the same medium as the GS papers; it is not a separate choice.
- Answers go in the Question-cum-Answer Booklet. Space for rough work is provided; UPSC's instruction is that any page or part of a page left blank, and all rough work, must be clearly struck off.
- 2013 was the last year with a single, longer essay (one of four topics, 250 marks). From 2014 the two-section format has been constant.

**How the paper has shifted**

- 2014–2018: a mix of issue-based prompts (policy paralysis, competition and youth, standardised tests, tourism, water disputes, cooperative federalism, jobless growth, farming, NAM, border management) and value-based or quotation prompts (character of an institution, education without values, "a good life is one inspired by love and guided by knowledge", "customary morality cannot be a guide to modern life"). Issue-based topics appeared in both sections; the sections were not divided by type.
- 2019–2020: Section A turned openly philosophical ("Wisdom finds truth"; "Values are not what humanity is, but what humanity ought to be"; "Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication"). Section B stayed issue-based (media, AI and jobs, primary health and education, patriarchy, technology in international relations).
- 2021–2024: both sections are dominated by aphorisms and literary or philosophical quotes ("The real is rational and the rational is real"; "You cannot step twice in the same river"; "Mathematics is the music of reason"; "The doubter is a true man of science"). At most one or two topics a year are plainly issue-based (social media and FOMO, 2024; girls' restrictions and boys' demands, 2023).
- Net effect: the learner must be able to **interpret** a quote, build a thesis around it, and still ground it in Indian and global examples. Memorised GS-topic essays (women, education, federalism) no longer map neatly onto the prompts.

**What high scorers do**

1. State a clear thesis early (by the end of the introduction) and keep every paragraph in service of it.
2. Cover many dimensions: historical, political, economic, social, technological, environmental, ethical, legal, international, personal. Not all ten, but five or six, each with a concrete example.
3. Use examples from history, science, literature, Indian society and the world, not only from current affairs. A philosophical topic needs one or two science or history examples to stop it floating.
4. Keep the flow coherent: each paragraph links back to the thesis and forward to the next. The examiner never has to ask "why is this paragraph here?"
5. Open with something memorable (an anecdote, a scene, a short definition) and close with a vision or a return to the opening, not with "thus, to conclude, the government should".
6. Hit the length: 1000–1200 words, typically 7–10 handwritten sides depending on handwriting. Finish both essays with time to re-read.

**What is punished**

- Listing GS points (schemes, articles, committees) under subheadings: it reads as a GS answer and ignores the prompt's idea.
- Moralising or preaching ("we must all become good citizens") in place of argument.
- One-sided essays: no acknowledgement of the counter-view, so the thesis is never tested.
- No thesis: the essay "discusses" without saying anything.
- Weak structure: paragraphs of unequal size, no transitions, a conclusion that merely repeats.
- Length errors: an 800-word essay looks unprepared; a 1600-word essay eats the time of the second essay and is itself often padded.
- Misinterpreting the quote. If the topic is "The real is rational and the rational is real", an essay on "real vs fake news" scores near zero on understanding.
- Factual errors in examples, invented quotes, misattributed quotes. One obvious fabrication damages trust in the whole essay.
- Mixing languages, slang, exclamation marks, bullet points inside the essay body (bullets belong in rough work only).

## Choosing the topic in the hall

Spend the first 10 minutes of the 3 hours on selection and outlining for **both** essays; the time is repaid many times over. Protocol:

1. **Minute 0–2: read all eight topics twice.** On the second pass, write one line under each: "what is this really asking?" For a quote, restate it in plain words ("You cannot step twice in the same river" = change is the only constant; identity persists through change).
2. **Minute 2–4: eliminate.** Strike any topic where (a) you cannot restate it confidently, (b) you can think of fewer than four examples, (c) you would only be able to argue one side. Keep two per section.
3. **Minute 4–5: choose by examples you have, not by interest.** For each surviving topic count examples on your fingers: history, science, India, world, personal. Pick the one with the most and the most varied. A topic that interests you but gives you two examples produces a thin essay; a plainer topic with nine examples produces a full one.
4. **Minute 5–8: brainstorm on the rough page with the dimensions wheel.** Write the topic in the centre and ten spokes: historical, political, economic, social, technological, environmental, ethical, legal, international, personal. Put one example or argument on each spoke that fits; leave the others blank. Add a spoke for the counter-view.
5. **Minute 8–10: outline 6–8 body paragraphs.** Order them: intro → context/definition → 4–6 dimension paragraphs (strongest first and last) → counter-view and response → conclusion. Write one line per paragraph: its claim and its example. Decide the intro device and the closing device now.
6. **Allocate time.** For each essay: 10 min selection and outline (already spent), 70 min writing, 5 min re-reading. Essay 1 done by 1:25, essay 2 by 2:50, 10 minutes spare. Write the clock targets on the rough page and check at each paragraph.

If a topic is a quote by a known author (Shelley, Marx, Hegel, Heraclitus, Churchill), name the author in the intro only if you are sure (see the attribution table above); otherwise treat it as an anonymous aphorism. Never guess an attribution in the hall.

## Structure that works

**Introductions (120–150 words; choose one device)**

- *Anecdote*: a short, real scene that embodies the idea (Galileo recanting before the Inquisition in 1633; Semmelweis ignored by his colleagues over handwashing in the 1840s; a Kerala fisherman steering his boat into flooded streets in August 2018). End with the thesis.
- *Quote with explanation*: a one-line quote, then two sentences unpacking it, then your thesis. Never open with the topic quote itself; it is already on the paper.
- *Definition*: define the key terms, show their tension ("justice is what is owed; charity is what is given"), and state the thesis.
- *Striking data*: one verifiable number (India's forest cover is a little over a fifth of its geographical area per the India State of Forest Report; 104 satellites in one ISRO launch in 2017), followed by why it matters. Check the figure against the latest edition before using it.
- *Scene*: a descriptive present-tense picture ("A girl in Bihar cycles to school on a government bicycle...") that the conclusion can return to.

**Body paragraphs (120–160 words each; 6–8 of them)**

Pattern: **Claim → Explanation → Example → Link.**
- Claim: the one-line point of the paragraph, in the first sentence.
- Explanation: why it is true, two or three sentences.
- Example: a concrete historical, scientific, Indian or global instance with a name and a date where possible.
- Link: a sentence tying the point back to the thesis and setting up the next paragraph.

Order dimensions so the essay moves: individual → society → nation → world, or past → present → future, or problem → cause → consequence → way forward.

**Transitions** (vary them, one per paragraph): "If the economic case is strong, the social case is stronger"; "History offers a warning here"; "Yet this picture is incomplete"; "The same logic applies, with greater force, to..."; "Move from the nation to the citizen and..."; "Science tells a similar story".

**Counter-view paragraph** (one, placed before the last body paragraph or the conclusion): state the strongest objection fairly, in its own voice ("Critics argue that..."), give it an example, then answer it without dismissing it ("This is true as far as it goes, but..."). An essay without this paragraph is marked down for being one-sided under both the depth and the understanding heads of the rubric below.

**Conclusions (100–130 words; choose one)**

- *Vision*: what the world or India looks like if the thesis is acted on; tie to constitutional values or the SDGs without listing them.
- *Return to the anecdote*: close the loop with the opening scene, now changed by the argument.
- *Balanced synthesis*: restate the thesis as a reconciliation of the two sides.
- *Quote*: a different, short, correctly attributed quote that crystallises the thesis, followed by one sentence of your own. Do not end on someone else's words alone.

**Model outline 1, philosophical: "The doubter is a true man of science" (2024; Claude Bernard)**

1. Intro (definition): doubt as method vs doubt as cynicism; thesis: organised doubt is what makes knowledge self-correcting, in science and beyond.
2. History of science: Copernicus and Galileo doubting geocentrism; Semmelweis and handwashing (1847); Einstein doubting Newtonian absolutes. Link: progress came from doubting the settled.
3. Method: Popper's falsifiability; a claim that cannot be doubted is not scientific. Example: the ozone-hole evidence that led to the Montreal Protocol (1987).
4. Indian tradition of doubt: Nachiketa's questions in the Katha Upanishad; the Buddha's advice in the Kalama Sutta to test teachings; Article 51A(h) and scientific temper. Link: doubt is not foreign to Indian culture.
5. Doubt in public life: RTI Act (2005) as institutionalised doubt of the state; peer review, audit, judicial review.
6. Society and technology: evidence-based policy (NFHS data on anaemia changing programme design); doubt as a defence against misinformation in the social-media age.
7. Counter-view: doubt without limit becomes denialism (vaccine hesitancy, climate denial); conspiracy thinking masquerades as doubt. Response: scientific doubt is disciplined by evidence and ends in provisional belief; cynicism ends nowhere.
8. Ethics and the personal: doubt as humility; Gandhi's "experiments with truth"; the leader who doubts listens.
9. Conclusion (vision): a republic of doubters who still act, from laboratory to panchayat.

**Model outline 2, issue-based: "Girls are weighed down by restrictions, boys with demands: two equally harmful disciplines" (2023)**

1. Intro (scene): two children in the same house at 6 pm, one told to come in, one told to go out and earn; thesis: patriarchy disciplines both sexes and both pay, though not equally.
2. Social: restrictions on girls' mobility, dress, timing; demands on boys to earn, not cry, protect. Example: NFHS-5 data on women's freedom to go alone to the market, a health facility or outside the village; NCRB (ADSI) data showing men account for the large majority of suicides. Update both from the latest release.
3. Economic: female labour-force participation far below men's (PLFS; update from the latest round); boys pushed out of education into work. Example: Bihar's bicycle scheme for girls raising secondary enrolment.
4. Historical: reform movements that worked on both (Savitribai and Jyotirao Phule's schools; Raja Rammohan Roy); the Constitution's Article 15(3).
5. Psychological / health: body-image pressure and eating disorders among girls; violence and substance abuse among boys; the mental-health cost of rigid norms on both.
6. Legal and institutional: laws and schemes aimed at girls' protection and opportunity (Prohibition of Child Marriage Act 2006; Beti Bachao Beti Padhao, 2015) but no comparable frame for boys' burdens; POCSO (2012) as an example of gender-neutral drafting; the case for gender-neutral policy design.
7. Technological: social media amplifying both scripts (filters for girls, hustle culture for boys).
8. Counter-view: the harms are not symmetric; restrictions on girls cost freedom and life chances in ways demands on boys do not. Response: "equally harmful" is best read as "both harmful", and the cure is the same: dismantle the script.
9. Conclusion (return to scene): the same house, where both children choose.

## Examples, quotes and data bank

Use at most one example per paragraph and name a date or person. Everything below is pre-2025. Any figure marked "update" must be checked against the latest edition of the source before it goes into an essay.

**Examples**

*Education and knowledge*
1. Kothari Commission (1964–66) opened its report with "the destiny of India is now being shaped in her classrooms"; UPSC used the line as a topic in 2017.
2. Tamil Nadu's noon-meal scheme (expanded under M.G. Ramachandran, 1982) and the Supreme Court's 2001 interim order in *PUCL v. Union of India* directing cooked mid-day meals in government schools: enrolment and attendance rose.
3. Kerala's total literacy campaign: Ernakulam district declared fully literate in 1990; the state in 1991; run through volunteers and local bodies.
4. ASER reports (Pratham, since 2005) showing enrolment near-universal but foundational reading and arithmetic weak: the gap between schooling and learning. Update the latest figures.
5. Bihar's Mukhyamantri Balika Cycle Yojana (2006) raised girls' secondary enrolment; studied by Muralidharan and Prakash.
6. Super 30 (Patna, Anand Kumar, from 2002) as a counter-example to the claim that coaching must be expensive.

*Democracy, polity, governance*
7. Constituent Assembly, 25 Nov 1949: Ambedkar's warning on the "grammar of anarchy", hero-worship, and political vs social democracy.
8. Emergency 1975–77 and the 1977 election: the electorate as the final check.
9. Right to Information Act 2005, born from the MKSS movement's jan sunwais in Rajasthan.
10. Kudumbashree (Kerala, 1998): women's self-help groups as local democracy and livelihood.
11. 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992, in force 1993): one-third reservation for women in local bodies, later raised to 50% by many states.
12. Odisha's "zero-casualty" policy for Cyclone Fani (2019), built after the 1999 super-cyclone: deaths in the tens against roughly ten thousand in 1999; state capacity built by learning from failure.
13. Supreme Court, *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017): privacy as a fundamental right; relevant to technology, media, surveillance essays.

*Economy and development*
14. 1991 reforms: balance-of-payments crisis, gold pledged, devaluation, delicensing; the growth and inequality debate since.
15. Operation Flood (1970, Verghese Kurien, NDDB/Amul): the cooperative model that made India the largest milk producer (update the current production figure).
16. Green Revolution (late 1960s, Norman Borlaug and M.S. Swaminathan): food security achieved, with later costs of groundwater depletion and soil health in Punjab.
17. Grameen Bank (Bangladesh, Muhammad Yunus; Nobel Peace Prize 2006, shared with the bank): microcredit and women's agency.
18. Bhutan's Gross National Happiness index; UPSC's 2013 GDP-vs-GDH prompt.
19. JAM trinity (Jan Dhan, Aadhaar, Mobile) and UPI: digital public infrastructure for inclusion; also the exclusion debate (biometric authentication failures in ration delivery).
20. Hiware Bazar and Ralegan Siddhi (Maharashtra): watershed-led village transformation.
21. Amartya Sen's observation (*Development as Freedom*, 1999, drawing on earlier work) that no substantial famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press.

*Technology and science*
22. Mangalyaan (launched 2013, Mars orbit 2014) reached Mars on the first attempt at a cost of roughly ₹450 crore; Chandrayaan-3 (2023) landed near the lunar south pole.
23. ISRO's 104 satellites in one launch (PSLV-C37, February 2017).
24. Fleming's penicillin (1928): discovery through attention to an accident; use for "magical in the mundane" and doubt/serendipity.
25. Haber–Bosch process: fed billions through fertiliser and also enabled explosives; the dual-use nature of technology.
26. Oppenheimer and the atomic bomb (1945): science outrunning ethics; "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds", his rendering of the Bhagavad Gita.
27. Cambridge Analytica (revealed 2018): data harvested from social media for political micro-targeting.
28. Arab Spring (2010–11): social media as mobiliser, and the later disillusion.
29. AlphaFold (2020) on protein-structure prediction; ChatGPT (Nov 2022) bringing generative AI to the public; the reskilling debate UPSC raised in 2019.
30. Covid-19: the CoWIN platform and the indigenous Covaxin; also the migrant exodus of 2020 as the limit of technology without welfare.

*Environment*
31. Chipko movement (1973, in present-day Uttarakhand): village women hugging trees; forests as livelihood, not only timber.
32. Montreal Protocol (1987): the global environmental treaty that demonstrably worked; the ozone layer is recovering.
33. Silent Valley (Kerala; project shelved 1983, national park 1984): a hydro project halted to save a rainforest, an early Indian conservation victory.
34. Bhopal gas disaster (1984): industrial safety, corporate accountability, environmental justice.
35. Ice stupas in Ladakh (Sonam Wangchuk, from 2013–14): frugal innovation for water in a warming Himalaya.
36. Costa Rica reversing deforestation through payments for ecosystem services (programme from 1997).
37. Delhi's winter air crisis and stubble burning: a collective-action failure across states.

*Women and society*
38. Savitribai and Jyotirao Phule's school for girls in Pune (1848).
39. Justice Verma Committee (report January 2013) after the December 2012 Delhi gang rape: law reform driven by public mobilisation (Criminal Law (Amendment) Act 2013).
40. Mary Kom, Mithali Raj, P.V. Sindhu; the Indian women's hockey team's fourth place at Tokyo 2020 (held 2021): sport as a lens on women's opportunity.
41. Rosalind Franklin's under-credited contribution to the DNA structure (1953); Janaki Ammal and Kamala Sohonie as Indian women scientists who worked against the odds.

*Ethics and character*
42. Gandhi withdrawing Non-Cooperation after Chauri Chaura (February 1922): means over ends, unpopular but principled.
43. Nelson Mandela and South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (hearings from 1996): justice as restoration, not revenge.
44. E. Sreedharan and the Delhi Metro: integrity and delivery in public works.
45. Satyendra Dubey (killed 2003) and Manjunath Shanmugam (killed 2005): whistle-blowers who paid with their lives; fed the debate that produced the Whistle Blowers Protection Act 2014.

## Practice plan

**Cadence** (Mains is typically held in September; confirm the year's calendar on upsc.gov.in)

- Month −6 to −4 (before Prelims or just after): read, not write. Build the example bank: 3 entries per weeknight (10 minutes) from GS reading, newspaper editorials and the list above, filed under the dimension wheel. Read two or three toppers' essays to see the shape, not to copy.
- Month −4 to −2 (typically from the Prelims result onward): **one full essay per week**, timed, handwritten, on a past topic. Alternate a philosophical and an issue-based prompt. Outline, but do not write, a second topic the same week (10 minutes).
- Last 6 weeks: **two essays per week**, both in one 3-hour sitting every second week to rehearse the full paper. Rotate across the eight families above so no family is untouched.
- Last week: no new essays. Re-read your five best outlines and the example bank; write one intro and one conclusion a day from memory.

**Self-evaluation (do it the next day, not the same evening)**

1. Underline the thesis. If you cannot find one sentence that states it, that is the first fix.
2. Number the dimensions covered. Fewer than five means thin.
3. Circle every example. Fewer than six, or more than two from the same domain, means unbalanced.
4. Read only the first sentence of every paragraph in sequence. If the essay still makes sense, the structure is fine; if not, the paragraph order or the claims need work.
5. Count words (average per line × lines). Note the time taken.
6. Write one line of self-feedback per rubric head below and keep a running sheet; the pattern across four essays is the real diagnosis.

**Using Claude for evaluation**

Send the topic, the full text (typed, or a legible photo transcribed) and the time taken. Claude applies the rubric in *Grading an essay* below and returns a band score out of 125, three strengths, three fixes quoting your sentences, and one rewritten paragraph. Ask for: "outline check" (before writing, to test a 6–8 paragraph skeleton), "intro/conclusion only" (10-minute drills), or "full grade". Keep every graded essay in one file so Claude can compare with the previous one and say what moved.

**Fitting it in as a working professional (~2 h weeknights, ~7 h each weekend day)**

- Saturday, first 90 minutes (fresh mind): write one timed essay, 15 minutes outline plus 75 minutes writing. Do it before GS work, because essays need energy, not time.
- Sunday, 30 minutes: self-evaluate with the checklist, submit for grading, read the feedback, rewrite the weakest paragraph by hand.
- In the two-essays-per-week phase: the second essay goes on Sunday morning; one Saturday in two becomes a full 3-hour mock (both essays).
- Weeknights: 10 minutes of example-bank entries after GS reading; one night a week, write only an intro and a conclusion for a past topic (20 minutes). This keeps the hand and the devices warm without a full sitting.
- Never trade the weekly essay for "one more GS chapter". In published marksheets the Essay paper shows one of the widest spreads between candidates of any Mains paper, so marks gained here are cheap relative to the time spent.

**For a full-timer**: the same cadence, but the weekly essay moves to a weekday morning and the second essay (in the last six weeks) to a second weekday; keep the weekend 3-hour mock every fortnight. Do not write more than two essays a week; the gain comes from evaluation and rewriting, not volume.

## Grading an essay

Score out of 125 per essay. Apply the weights below to a 0–100 scale, then convert, and report both the score and the band. The bands are this skill's internal calibration, not a UPSC scale, and are deliberately conservative. Treat the 40–60 range as the normal starting point for a first-time writer and say so early; that is what moves them.

**Rubric (weights)**

| Head | Weight | What earns full marks | What loses marks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Understanding of topic and thesis | 20% | The prompt's idea is interpreted correctly and fully; a thesis is stated by the end of the intro and the essay argues it | Topic misread; keyword-matching without grasping the idea; "discusses" with no position; thesis appears only in the conclusion |
| Structure and flow | 20% | Intro, 6–8 proportioned body paragraphs, counter-view, conclusion; each paragraph has one claim; transitions carry the reader; first sentences alone tell the story | Paragraphs of 30 and 300 words; no transitions; repetition; subheadings and bullets; conclusion that merely restates |
| Multi-dimensionality and depth | 25% | Five or more dimensions, each developed (why, so what), not merely named; the counter-view is taken seriously; the argument builds | A GS answer in disguise; one dimension stretched; generalities ("in today's world"); a list of points with no analysis |
| Examples and evidence | 15% | Six or more concrete, accurate examples across history, science, India and the world; each tied to the claim it supports | Vague examples ("many countries have done this"); wrong facts; invented quotes; examples that do not prove the claim; all examples from one domain |
| Language and expression | 10% | Clear, plain English; varied sentence length; no grammar slips that obscure meaning; appropriate register | Jargon, slang, rhetorical questions in a row, exclamation marks, mixed language, clichés ("double-edged sword") used as argument |
| Introduction and conclusion | 10% | Intro hooks and states the thesis; conclusion offers a vision or synthesis and links back to the opening | Intro that restates the topic; conclusion that lists government schemes or preaches; abrupt or missing ending |

**Bands (out of 125; internal calibration)**

- **Below 40**: topic misread or no thesis; GS points under headings; few or wrong examples; under 800 words. Feedback must start with interpretation, not polish.
- **40–50**: topic understood but argued thinly; 3–4 dimensions, several unsupported; examples generic; structure visible but mechanical; conclusion restates. Where most first practice essays sit.
- **50–60**: clear thesis; 5+ dimensions, some developed; several concrete examples, uneven spread; counter-view present but brief; language competent; intro or conclusion weak.
- **60–70**: thesis argued throughout; dimensions developed with accurate, varied examples; counter-view fairly stated and answered; flow natural; memorable intro and a conclusion that adds something.
- **70+**: everything above plus originality of interpretation, examples the examiner has not seen a hundred times, economy of language, and a shape the reader can recall afterwards. Rare. Never translate a band into a promised UPSC score; actual marks vary by year and examiner, and the only reliable reference is published marksheets.

**How to give feedback**

1. **Score and band first**, one line, with the per-head breakdown. Say what one change would move it to the next band.
2. **Three strengths**, each quoting a sentence or paragraph from the essay so the learner knows what to keep.
3. **Three fixes**, in priority order (interpretation and thesis before structure before examples before language), each quoting the offending sentence and stating the replacement principle, not just "improve this".
4. **One rewritten paragraph**: take the weakest body paragraph, keep the learner's claim and example, and rewrite it to length in the claim–explanation–example–link pattern. Show it beside the original.
5. **What to do tonight**: one 20-minute task derived from fix 1 (rewrite the intro; outline the same topic from the opposite thesis; add three examples to the bank for the thinnest dimension).
6. Keep the tone of a senior examiner: direct, specific, no praise padding, no sarcasm. If facts or quotes in the essay are wrong, say so plainly and give the correct version; if an attribution is unverifiable, tell them to drop or soften it.
