# GS Paper I — Resource Map (Mains)

Scope of this file: Mains GS Paper I only (History, Art & Culture, Society, Geography). Prelims chapter maps for the same subjects live in the Prelims subject files; do not re-teach them from here. Exam mechanics (typically 20 questions: 10 of 10 marks at ~150 words and 10 of 15 marks at ~250 words; 250 marks; 3 hours; English or any Eighth Schedule language) are in exam-facts; always confirm the current year's notice on upsc.gov.in. UPSC does not publish a question-wise or topic-wise marks distribution, and there are no published cutoffs for individual papers; everything below about "weight" is a reading of the 2013-2024 papers, not an official figure.

Working rule for this paper: GS I rewards **named specifics** (person, place, act, year, scheme, data point), **one diagram or map** wherever geography permits, and **balanced analysis** that goes beyond narration. Pure narrative answers score poorly even when factually correct.

## Syllabus, decoded

The official syllabus lines, what the examiner usually wants, qualitative weight (based on the 2013-2024 papers; the mix shifts year to year), and paraphrased PYQ themes. Years are given only where the question is well known; a theme without a year has appeared but the year should be checked against the paper.

### 1. Indian culture — salient aspects of art forms, literature and architecture, ancient to modern
- **What the examiner wants:** mostly descriptive-with-a-point. "Discuss/Elucidate" on one school, period or form; the better answers name 4-6 specific monuments/texts/artists and end with significance or legacy. Occasional analytical hooks ("represent social life", "owed to Romans as much as Greeks").
- **Weight:** moderate and steady; typically 2-3 questions a year, often one 10-marker and one 15-marker.
- **PYQ themes:** Tandava in early inscriptions and Chola temple architecture as a culmination (2013); Gandhara sculpture's Roman and Greek debts, Taxila vs Nalanda as a "university", IVC urban planning as input to present-day urbanisation (2014); Mesolithic rock art's aesthetic sense, continuity of Indian civilisation unlike Egypt/Mesopotamia/Greece (2015); early Buddhist stupa art and folk motifs, Krishnadeva Raya as scholar-patron (2016); Gupta numismatic excellence not matched later (2017); safeguarding art heritage, Chinese and Arab travellers' accounts as sources, Chaitanya's re-orientation of Bhakti (2018); Central Asian and Greco-Bactrian elements in Gandhara (2019); rock-cut architecture as a source of history, Pala period and Buddhism, Indian philosophy and tradition shaping monuments, Persian literary sources reflecting the spirit of the age (2020); nature of Bhakti literature and its contribution (2021); medieval temple sculpture and social life, Gupta and Chola contributions to heritage, lion and bull figures in mythology/art (2022); geographical factors in ancient India's development, Vedic society and religion and what survives, technological changes of the Sultanate period (2023); Rig Vedic to later Vedic changes in society and economy, Pallava contribution to South Indian art and literature, the "Purvaiya" monsoon wind and the cultural ethos of the Bhojpur region (2024).

### 2. Modern Indian history (mid-18th century to present) — significant events, personalities, issues
- **What the examiner wants:** analytical. "Why", "to what extent", "assess", "evaluate" dominate; the question usually contains a thesis to be tested (e.g. "Dalhousie founder of modern India").
- **Weight:** high and steady; typically 2-4 questions a year counting the freedom-struggle line below.
- **PYQ themes:** Dalhousie as founder of modern India (2013); British economic policies from mid-18th century to independence, the naval mutiny as the last nail (2014); mid-18th century fragmented polity, decline of artisanal industry and the rural economy (2017); 1857 as culmination of a century of local rebellions (2019); Curzon's policies and their long-term implications (2020); why East India Company armies won consistently, sudden spurt in famines since the mid-18th century (2022); colonial impact on tribals and tribal responses (2023).

### 3. The freedom struggle — stages and contributors/contributions from different parts of the country
- **What the examiner wants:** analytical plus regional breadth. Questions test phases (moderate, extremist, Gandhian), comparative leadership, and "many voices" (women, foreigners, peasants, tribals, princely-state subjects, the South and North-East).
- **Weight:** high; nearly every year carries at least one question here, often a 15-marker.
- **PYQ themes:** women as torch-bearers, foreigners in the movement (2013); world developments that motivated the anti-colonial struggle (2014); independence without Gandhi, Gandhi vs Ambedkar sharing a goal (2015); 1857 as a watershed in British policy, women in the Gandhian phase, Bose vs Gandhi (2016); why the Moderates failed to carry conviction by the end of the 19th century, new objectives added to the vision of independence from the 1920s, the women's question in 19th-century reform (2017); relevance of Gandhi's thought today, indentured labour and cultural identity abroad (2018); 19th-century "Indian Renaissance" and national identity, many voices in the Gandhian phase, the British role in complicating transfer of power in the 1940s (2019); ideological strands and expanding social base since the 1920s (2020); Moderates preparing the base, Gandhi's constructive programme in NCM and CDM, Young Bengal and Brahmo Samaj (2021); Gandhi vs Tagore on education and nationalism (2023); events leading to Quit India and its results (2024).

### 4. Post-independence consolidation and reorganisation within the country
- **What the examiner wants:** analytical with chronology; integration of states, linguistic reorganisation, tribal integration, early foreign-policy and agrarian choices, and "is it still continuing" framings.
- **Weight:** low to moderate; 0-2 questions a year, heavier in 2013 than since.
- **PYQ themes:** Bhoodan-Gramdan, "Jai Jawan Jai Kisan", Maulana Azad's contribution, Tashkent Agreement, India's role in the emergence of Bangladesh (2013); Lenin's NEP and policies adopted soon after independence (2014); Constituent Assembly and the 1935 Act experience (2015); linguistic states and Indian unity (2016); administrative and socio-cultural problems in integrating princely states (2021); reorganisation of states and territories as a continuous process since the mid-19th century (2022).

### 5. History of the world (from 18th century): industrial revolution, world wars, redrawal of boundaries, colonisation, decolonisation, political philosophies (communism, capitalism, socialism) and their effects on society
- **What the examiner wants:** analytical, comparative, often with a link to India or to "effects on society". Short, thesis-driven, example-rich answers.
- **Weight:** was moderate (2-4 questions) in 2013-2015, then thin; many papers since 2020 carry zero or one question. Prepare for breadth, not depth.
- **PYQ themes:** Japan's "latecomer" industrial revolution, Africa "chopped into states", American Revolution as revolt against mercantilism, policy instruments against the Great Depression (2013); Suez Crisis 1956 and Britain's self-image, Lenin's NEP (2014); why the industrial revolution began in England and quality of life then vs India now, Germany's responsibility for the two world wars (2015); West African anti-colonial struggles led by Western-educated elites (2016); decolonisation problems in the Malay Peninsula (2017); American and French Revolutions laying foundations of the modern world (2019); socio-economic effects of railways in different countries (2023).

### 6. Salient features of Indian society; diversity of India
- **What the examiner wants:** analytical with sociological vocabulary (unity in diversity, pluralism, syncretism, caste as fluid/static, "cultural pockets"), plus contemporary illustrations.
- **Weight:** moderate; usually 1-2 questions.
- **PYQ themes:** four cultural elements of diversity and national identity (2015); globalisation and the core of cultural diversity (2016); regions as cultural units rather than states, tolerance and love as features of Indian society (2017); what makes Indian society unique in sustaining culture, "cultural pockets of small India" (2019); caste's relevance in a multicultural society, diversity and pluralism under threat from globalisation (2020); continuity of traditional social values and ongoing changes, uniqueness of tribal knowledge systems (2021); salience of "sect" vis-a-vis caste, region and religion; tribal communities as a single category (2022); caste identity fluid and static, marriage as sacrament losing value, mobile phones replacing child cuddling and socialisation (2023); inter-caste vs inter-religious marriage and socio-economic parity, cultural diversity and socio-economic marginality (2024).

### 7. Role of women and women's organisations
- **What the examiner wants:** analytical, data-backed, with a historical arc (19th-century reform to present) and a critical edge (whose issues the women's movement addresses).
- **Weight:** steady; typically one question a year.
- **PYQ themes:** male membership in women's organisations (2013); patriarchy and the middle-class working woman, adverse sex ratio in prosperous regions, feminisation of agriculture (2014); positive and negative effects of globalisation on women (2015); women in the freedom struggle (2016); women's question in 19th-century reform (2017); women's movement and women of lower strata (2018); empowering women as the key to population control, continued challenges across time and space (2019); rising suicide among young women (2023); gender equality vs equity vs empowerment and gender-aware programme design; gig economy and women's empowerment (2024).

### 8. Population and associated issues
- **What the examiner wants:** data (Census, NFHS, SRS) plus a social explanation; migration, sex ratio, ageing, demographic dividend/winter, population education.
- **Weight:** steady; one question most years.
- **PYQ themes:** globalisation and the aged (2013); sex ratio among tribes vs Scheduled Castes, population-poverty causality, labour migration trends over four decades (2015); population education objectives and measures (2021); why large cities attract more migrants than small towns, "demographic winter" (2024).

### 9. Poverty and developmental issues
- **What the examiner wants:** conceptual (deprivation, capability, inequality) with data and a scheme or two; increasingly tied to human development and digital access.
- **Weight:** low to moderate; 0-1 question a year.
- **PYQ themes:** liberating the poor from the process of deprivation (2016); aspirational districts strategy (2018); COVID-19 accelerating class inequality and poverty (2020); human development lagging economic development, status and problems of digitalisation (2023); government-NGO-private collaboration on socio-economic development (2024).

### 10. Urbanisation — problems and remedies
- **What the examiner wants:** problem-remedy structure with named programmes (Smart Cities Mission, AMRUT, PMAY-U, Metro Rail Policy 2017) and city examples; flooding and segregation are favourites.
- **Weight:** steady; one question most years, sometimes framed from the geography side.
- **PYQ themes:** social problems from speedy urbanisation, urban heat islands (2013); Delhi's worse air pollution vs Mumbai and Kolkata (2015); Smart City objectives, major cities vulnerable to floods (2016); IT-hub cities and new problems (2017); urban water harvesting (2018); flooding of million-plus cities including Hyderabad and Pune (2020); IT industries and socio-economic implications in major cities, reclaiming water bodies for urban land use (2021); Tier-2 cities and a consumption-oriented middle class (2022); urbanisation and segregation/marginalisation of the poor in metropolises (2023); large cities and migration (2024).

### 11. Effects of globalisation on Indian society
- **What the examiner wants:** both-sides analysis anchored in sociological ideas (homogenisation vs glocalisation, cultural specificity, identity), with examples from food, media, family, work.
- **Weight:** steady; one question most years.
- **PYQ themes:** globalisation and the aged (2013); globalisation and women (2015); globalisation and cultural diversity (2016); homogenisation strengthening cultural specificities (2018); diversity and pluralism under threat (2020); globalisation and new technology under scarce resources, work-from-home and family relationships (2022); post-liberal economy, ethnic identity and communalism (2023).

### 12. Social empowerment, communalism, regionalism and secularism
- **What the examiner wants:** definition-manifestation-cause-impact-remedy; comparative with the West for secularism; specific illustrations (events, movements, constitutional provisions).
- **Weight:** moderate; 1-2 questions a year.
- **PYQ themes:** regionalism and demand for separate states (2013); Indian vs Western debates on secularism (2014); Dalit assertion and annihilation of caste (2015); basis of regionalism and unequal development, Scheduled Tribes and constitutional provisions (2016); religiosity vs communalism with an example of transformation (2017); communalism from power struggle or relative deprivation, caste assuming new associational forms (2018); challenges to cultural practices in the name of secularism (2019); customs and traditions suppressing reason (2020); tolerance, assimilation and pluralism as elements of Indian secularism (2022); post-liberal economy and communalism (2023).

### 13. Salient features of the world's physical geography
- **What the examiner wants:** mechanism-first explanations with a diagram; climatology and oceanography dominate, geomorphology next.
- **Weight:** high; typically 2-4 questions a year.
- **PYQ themes:** continental drift evidence, temperature inversion, hot deserts on western margins at 20-30 degrees N, tropical cyclone naming (2013); El Nino and unusual climate, fold mountains along continental margins with earthquake-volcano association, island formation in Indonesia and the Philippines, cyclones confined to the South China Sea/Bay of Bengal/Gulf of Mexico (2014); origin of ocean currents and effects on climate, fishing, navigation (2015); air masses and macro-climate (2016); ocean salinity variations and effects, cryosphere and global climate (2017); mantle plumes and plate tectonics, dead zones (2018); ocean currents vs water masses (2019); Circum-Pacific zone, desertification without climatic boundaries (2020); alignment of mountain ranges and local weather, Arctic ice vs Antarctic glacier melt, 2021 volcanic eruptions (2021); primary rocks, troposphere and weather, forces behind ocean currents and fisheries, straits and isthmuses in trade (2022); fjord formation (2023); sea-surface temperature rise and tropical cyclones, cloudbursts, auroras (2024).

### 14. Distribution of key natural resources across the world (including South Asia and India)
- **What the examiner wants:** distribution pattern plus implications; water, minerals, oil, ocean resources, coastline, Arctic.
- **Weight:** moderate; 1-2 questions.
- **PYQ themes:** atomic-energy raw materials, shale oil and gas reserves (2013); ocean resources for the resource crisis (2014); fresh water endowment yet scarcity, Arctic oil discovery significance (2015); micro-watershed development in drought-prone regions, Indus Waters Treaty implications (2016); water stress and regional variation (2019); Himalayan glacier melt and India's water resources (2020); Gondwanaland and mining's low GDP share, uneven distribution of mineral oil (2021); Deccan Trap resource potential, wind-energy potential and limited spread (2022); freshwater crisis of availability and access, resource potential of India's coastline and hazard preparedness, diversity of natural vegetation (2023).

### 15. Factors responsible for the location of primary, secondary and tertiary sector industries (world and India)
- **What the examiner wants:** classical location factors (raw material, market, energy, transport, labour, policy, agglomeration) applied to a named industry, then "why the pattern is changing".
- **Weight:** moderate; usually one question a year.
- **PYQ themes:** sugar mills shifting south, decentralised cotton textiles (2013); tea beyond Darjeeling, spatial shift of world iron and steel (2014); petroleum refineries away from oil fields (2017); industrial corridors, blue revolution and pisciculture (2018); regional-resource-based manufacturing for employment, agro-processing in North-West India (2019); iron and steel locating away from raw materials (2020); IT industries in major cities (2021).

### 16. Important geophysical phenomena (earthquakes, tsunami, volcanoes, cyclones) and geographical features and their location
- **What the examiner wants:** process, global/Indian distribution, impact, and management; a diagram is almost mandatory.
- **Weight:** high; 1-3 questions a year.
- **PYQ themes:** landslides in the Himalaya vs the Western Ghats (2013, 2021); Himalayan landslide causes and mitigation (2016); Phailin and cyclone naming (2013); fold mountain, earthquake and volcano belts (2014); floods as a resource for irrigation and navigation (2017); IMD colour-coded cyclone warnings (2022); cloudbursts, SST and cyclogenesis (2024). (The National Landslide Risk Management Strategy, NDMA 2019, is a useful reference for the 2021-type landslide question.)

### 17. Changes in critical geographical features (water bodies, ice caps, flora and fauna) and their effects
- **What the examiner wants:** evidence of change (named glacier, lake, reef, mangrove), causal chain, and consequences for people; links to climate change and to GS III disaster management.
- **Weight:** moderate and rising since 2019.
- **PYQ themes:** shrinking Himalayan glaciers and climate change, no deltas on Western Ghats rivers (2013); Western Himalayan states and tourism carrying capacity, changing monsoon and humanised landscapes (2015); mountain ecosystem restoration, mangrove depletion, corals under warming (2019); Himalayan glacier melt and water resources (2020); Arctic vs Antarctic melt effects, water-body reclamation (2021); climate change and food security in tropical countries (2023).

## Answer frameworks that work in GS I

Every answer: a 2-3 line introduction that defines or dates the subject, a body in headed sections (not a wall of prose), a diagram or map wherever geography allows, and a conclusion that answers the directive word (balanced for "critically", forward-looking for "way forward"). Within 150/250 words, the body should carry 4-6 distinct, named points.

### 1. Causes — Course — Consequences (+ one historiographical line) — for events
Use for 1857, Swadeshi, NCM, CDM, Quit India, Partition, Suez, Russian Revolution.
Skeleton ("What were the events that led to the Quit India Movement? Point out its results", 2024):
- Intro: 8 August 1942, AICC Bombay session, "Do or Die", context of the Second World War.
- Causes: failure of the Cripps Mission (March-April 1942); war-time hardship and fear of Japanese advance after the fall of Burma; price rise, requisitioning and the scorched-earth policy in Bengal; collapse of British prestige; Gandhi's shift to "ordered anarchy" and the Wardha CWC resolution (July 1942).
- Course (two lines, since the question stresses causes and results): leaders arrested, leaderless mass phase, parallel governments (Ballia, Tamluk, Satara), underground network (Aruna Asaf Ali, Achyut Patwardhan, Usha Mehta's Congress Radio).
- Results: demonstrated that Congress could not be excluded from any settlement; British realisation after the war (Wavell's assessments, the Cabinet Mission); social spread to rural youth; but also the Muslim League's gains in the vacuum and the repression precedent.
- Historiographical line: Bipan Chandra's "struggle-truce-struggle" reading vs the "spontaneous upsurge" reading.
- Conclusion: link to 1945-47 (INA trials, RIN mutiny, transfer of power).

### 2. Context — Content — Contribution — Critique — for personalities, movements, policies
Use for Curzon, Dalhousie, Brahmo Samaj, Moderates, Gandhi's constructive programme, Bose vs Gandhi (as a two-column comparison).
Skeleton ("To what extent did the role of the Moderates prepare a base for the wider freedom movement?", 2021):
- Context: 1885-1905, who they were (Naoroji, Gokhale, Banerjea, Mehta), their methods (petitions, press, constitutional agitation).
- Content: demands (legislative reform, Indianisation of the civil service, military expenditure, separation of judicial and executive functions).
- Contribution: economic critique of colonialism (drain theory; Naoroji's *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India*, 1901); an all-India political platform; secular, democratic idiom; training of the next generation; legal gains (Indian Councils Act 1892).
- Critique: narrow social base, "political mendicancy", neglect of the masses and of peasant issues; failure to lead the Swadeshi upsurge.
- Conclusion weighing "extent": a base in ideas and institutions, not in mass mobilisation; Gandhi built on the first and supplied the second.

### 3. Nature — Features — Significance — Critique/Legacy — for art forms, architecture, literature
Use for Gandhara, Chola temples, Pallava art, stupa art, Bhakti literature, Persian sources, Mughal painting.
Skeleton ("Estimate the contribution of Pallavas of Kanchi for the development of art and literature of South India", 2024):
- Nature: 6th-9th century, Kanchipuram as capital, transition from rock-cut to structural stone.
- Features in stages: Mahendravarman I's rock-cut mandapas (Mandagapattu); Narasimhavarman I's monolithic rathas and the Descent of the Ganga relief at Mamallapuram; Rajasimha's (Narasimhavarman II) structural temples (Shore Temple, Kailasanatha at Kanchi); Nandivarman II phase (Vaikuntha Perumal).
- Literature and learning: Sanskrit and Tamil patronage (Bharavi and Dandin associated with the court; Mahendravarman I's *Mattavilasa Prahasana*), Kanchi as a seat of learning, the Alvars and Nayanars in Pallava country.
- Significance/legacy: Dravida vimana prototype adopted by the Cholas; spread to South-East Asia via maritime links; the Tamil Bhakti corpus.
- Critique/limit: smaller structural scale than the Chola; dependence on royal patronage.
- Conclusion with "estimate": foundational rather than culminating.

### 4. Then — Now — Why it persists/changed — for "continuity" questions
Use for Vedic features still prevailing (2023), IVC inputs to present-day urbanisation (2014), Gandhi's thought today (2018), tolerance as a continuing feature (2017).
Skeleton ("What are the main features of Vedic society and religion? Do you think some of the features are still prevailing in Indian society?", 2023):
- Then (Rig Vedic vs later Vedic, 4-5 features): tribal kin-based polity to territorial kingdoms; varna from flexible to hereditary; pastoral to agrarian economy; sacrifice-centred religion, rising priestly authority; position of women (from participation in sabha to restrictions).
- Now (map each): yajna/homa in rituals, gotra exogamy, varna-jati endogamy, the ashrama ideal, Sanskrit liturgy, Upanishadic thought in modern philosophy.
- Why: religious transmission, caste as a social institution, legal recognition of custom, modern reinterpretation (Arya Samaj's "Back to the Vedas").
- Conclusion: continuity in symbols and institutions, change in substance.

### 5. Meaning — Manifestations — Causes — Impact — Measures — for society and polity-society topics
Use for communalism, regionalism, caste, secularism, patriarchy, urban segregation.
Skeleton ("'Communalism arises either due to power struggle or relative deprivation.' Argue by giving suitable illustrations", 2018):
- Meaning: Bipan Chandra's three stages (shared secular interests of a religious community; those interests seen as different from other communities'; those interests seen as hostile); distinguish from religiosity.
- Manifestations: riots, vote mobilisation, communal organisations, everyday discrimination.
- Causes under the two heads in the question: power struggle (Partition politics, electoral competition, elite mobilisation, 1980s examples) vs relative deprivation (economic competition in Moradabad brassware 1980, Bhagalpur 1989, urban migrants); add a third head the question misses (colonial legacy, communal historiography, social media).
- Impact: one line each on security, economy, social capital, minority ghettoisation.
- Measures: law (hate-speech and riot provisions now in the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023; the lapsed Prevention of Communal and Targeted Violence Bill 2011), police reform, inter-community institutions (Ashutosh Varshney's civic-engagement finding in *Ethnic Conflict and Civic Life*), education, economic inclusion.
- Conclusion: both causes operate, mediated by politics.

### 6. Concept — Data — Debate — Way forward — for women, population, poverty, human development
Use for sex ratio, demographic winter, feminisation of agriculture, suicide among young women, human development lag.
Skeleton ("What is the concept of a 'demographic winter'? Is the world moving towards such a situation?", 2024):
- Concept: sustained below-replacement fertility (TFR under about 2.1) leading to shrinking, ageing populations; a term from demographic and policy debate, not an official UN classification.
- Data: Japan, South Korea, Italy; China's population decline from 2022; India NFHS-5 TFR 2.0 with state variation (Bihar about 3.0, Uttar Pradesh 2.4; southern states about 1.7-1.8; verify the latest).
- Debate: "is the world moving" — yes in high-income countries and East Asia, no in sub-Saharan Africa; UN *World Population Prospects 2024* projects a global peak in the 2080s (verify); India's demographic-dividend window vs regional ageing (Kerala, Tamil Nadu).
- Impact: dependency ratio, labour supply, pensions, migration politics.
- Way forward: family-friendly policy (childcare, parental leave), female labour-force participation, healthy ageing, managed migration; for India, invest in human capital in the high-fertility states.
- Conclusion: uneven, not universal; policy should be regionally differentiated.

### 7. Mechanism — Distribution/Regional pattern — Impact — Way forward (with diagram) — for physical geography and geophysical phenomena
Use for cyclones, cloudbursts, landslides, monsoon, ocean currents, fjords, auroras, volcanoes.
Skeleton ("What is sea surface temperature rise? How does it affect the formation of tropical cyclones?", 2024):
- Define SST and the warming trend (the ocean absorbs most of the excess heat in the climate system; the Indian Ocean has warmed faster than the global average; cite the latest IPCC/IMD figure).
- Mechanism of cyclogenesis: SST above about 26-27 degrees C through a 50-60 m layer, Coriolis force beyond about 5 degrees latitude, low vertical wind shear, a pre-existing disturbance, upper-level divergence. Draw a cross-section of a tropical cyclone (eye, eye wall, spiral bands, inflow and outflow).
- How SST rise alters it: more latent heat, rapid intensification (Arabian Sea cases such as Tauktae 2021, Biparjoy 2023), longer season, poleward shift, higher storm surge with sea-level rise, slower-moving systems with more rain; note that frequency change is less certain than intensity change.
- Regional pattern: Bay of Bengal still more frequent; the Arabian Sea share rising.
- Impact and way forward: IMD colour codes and impact-based forecasting, early warning and evacuation (the Odisha model since 1999/2013), coastal zoning, mangroves.

### 8. Factors — Regional pattern — Change over time — Implications — for resources and industrial location
Use for iron and steel, refineries, cotton textiles, sugar, agro-processing, IT, wind energy, mining.
Skeleton ("Account for the present location of iron and steel industries away from the source of raw material, by giving examples", 2020):
- Classical factors (Weber's least-cost model): weight-losing raw materials pulled plants to coal-iron belts (Chhotanagpur: Jamshedpur, Bokaro, Durgapur, Rourkela; the Ruhr; Pittsburgh).
- Change over time: imported ore and coking coal, bulk carriers and port economics (Visakhapatnam; Japanese coastal plants; Baosteel at Shanghai); electric-arc and scrap-based mini-mills near markets; sponge iron; technology reducing coal per tonne; markets and agglomeration; policy (SEZs, state incentives).
- Regional pattern now: coastal and market-oriented plants alongside the old belt; sketch a map of India with the Chhotanagpur belt and the coastal plants.
- Implications: logistics, employment shift, environmental load on ports, raw-material security.
- Conclusion: location is now a multi-factor transport-cost-and-market calculation, not a raw-material one.

Comparison questions (Bose vs Gandhi; Himalayan vs Western Ghats landslides; Arctic vs Antarctic melt; gender equality vs equity vs empowerment) use any of the above but present the body as a two- or three-column table with 4-5 criteria rows, then a short synthesis paragraph.

## What UPSC asked, 2013-2024 (patterns)

- **Thesis-testing on a named verdict** ("Dalhousie founder of modern India" 2013; "Gandhara owed as much to Romans as Greeks" 2014; "1857 the culmination of local rebellions" 2019; "Moderates prepared the base" 2021): take a position early, support with 3-4 named specifics, concede one counter-point, conclude.
- **Comparative leadership or approach** (Gandhi vs Ambedkar 2015; Bose vs Gandhi 2016; Gandhi vs Tagore 2023): tabular body on 4-5 criteria, then synthesis; avoid hagiography.
- **"Many voices" and regional contributors** (foreigners 2013; women 2013, 2016; indentured labour 2018; many voices in the Gandhian phase 2019; tribals 2023): the examiner is checking the syllabus phrase "from different parts of the country"; name the North-East, the South, princely states, peasants and workers.
- **Colonial economy and its social fallout** (economic policies 2014; artisanal decline 2017; famines 2022; railways across countries 2023): need a causal chain plus two or three numbers or named famines/commissions (1770 Bengal, 1876-78, 1896-1900, 1943; the Famine Commissions of 1880, 1898, 1901).
- **Art question anchored to one school or period** (Chola 2013; stupa art 2016; Gupta coins 2017; Gandhara 2019; rock-cut 2020; Gupta and Chola 2022; Pallava 2024): at least four named monuments or objects; one line on patronage and one on legacy; a quick plan sketch of a temple or stupa is acceptable and useful.
- **Culture as a source of history** (travellers 2018; rock-cut 2020; Persian sources 2020; temple sculpture and social life 2022): answer "what we learn" with examples, then the limits of the source.
- **Then-and-now continuity** (IVC 2014; civilisational continuity 2015; tolerance 2017; Gandhi today 2018; Vedic features 2023): equal weight to both halves; present-day examples must be concrete.
- **Post-independence integration and reorganisation** (Tashkent, Bangladesh 2013; linguistic states 2016; princely states 2021; reorganisation since the mid-19th century 2022): chronology plus assessment; mention the SRC, Article 3, recent cases (2000, 2014, 2019).
- **World history: causes of revolutions and their global effect; decolonisation case studies** (American/French 2013, 2019; England's industrial lead 2015; Germany's war guilt 2015; West Africa 2016; Malaya 2017; Suez 2014): one or two questions at most; prepare 10 one-page notes rather than a book.
- **Society: caste's changing forms** (Dalit assertion 2015; caste's new associational forms 2018; relevance in a multicultural society 2020; sect 2022; fluid and static 2023; inter-caste marriage 2024): use Srinivas (sanskritisation, dominant caste), Beteille, Rudolph and Rudolph on caste associations, and IHDS/NFHS inter-caste marriage figures; always give both continuity and change.
- **Society: women with data** (patriarchy 2014; sex ratio 2014, 2015; globalisation 2015; lower-strata women 2018; continued challenges 2019; suicide 2023; equality-equity-empowerment 2024): define the concept, give two numbers with source, one legal provision, one scheme, one critique.
- **Urban problems framed through a current event** (Phailin 2013; Delhi air 2015; Hyderabad and Pune floods 2020; WFH 2022; Tier-2 consumption 2022; migration to large cities 2024): the news hook is the entry point; the answer is still the syllabus (causes-impact-remedies with named programmes).
- **Secularism and communalism, India vs the West** (2014, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2022): the Indian model (principled distance, positive secularism, Articles 25-30) vs wall-of-separation; use Rajeev Bhargava's framing and one or two cases (S.R. Bommai 1994 on secularism as basic structure).
- **Physical geography: mechanism plus a current trigger** (El Nino 2014; Arctic and Antarctic melt 2021; 2021 volcanic eruptions; SST and cyclones 2024; cloudbursts 2024; auroras 2024): the diagram is where the marks sit; follow with impact on India.
- **Industrial location and resource geography of India** (sugar, cotton 2013; tea 2014; refineries 2017; iron and steel 2020; mining and Gondwanaland 2021; wind 2022): factors, map, change over time, policy.

**10-marker (about 150 words) expects:** a one-line definition or date, 3-4 headed points each with a named example or figure, one small diagram if geography, a one-line conclusion. Breadth over depth; no sub-sub-points.
**15-marker (about 250 words) expects:** the above plus a second dimension (critique, regional variation, change over time, or way forward), 5-6 points, a diagram and/or map where applicable, and a conclusion that explicitly answers the directive ("to what extent", "critically", "justify"). For "critically examine" questions, the two sides should be visibly separated.

## Teaching and grading in this paper

### Fitting GS I into the week
- Working professional (~2 h on weeknights, ~7 h on each weekend day, about 24 h/week): GS I gets roughly one weeknight and one weekend half-day per week in the Mains phase, i.e. 5-6 h; in those hours cover one syllabus line (one 60-90 minute teaching slot plus one timed answer) and one 20-minute revision of an earlier line. World history and the never-asked corners are weekend-only, 20-minute skims.
- Full-timer (about 55 h/week): two GS I slots a day in the Mains phase are plenty; the extra time goes to a second timed answer and the diagram/map drills, not to more sources.
- Either way: the primary source in the table is read once; everything after that is PYQ-driven note-making and answer writing.

### Teaching a GS I topic (60-90 minute slot)
Order and timing for a weeknight 2-hour window or one weekend block:
1. **PYQ first (5-10 min).** Show the learner every PYQ on the topic from the lists above; derive the two or three angles UPSC actually tests. This defines the depth; nothing outside those angles is taught at full length.
2. **Core from the primary source (25-35 min).** Teach from the exact chapter named in the sources table, in the examiner's order: for history, context-content-contribution-critique; for culture, nature-features-significance; for society, concept-manifestation-cause-impact-measure; for geography, mechanism-distribution-impact. Insist on named specifics: at least five names, dates or places per topic that the learner writes down.
3. **Diagram, map or data (10-15 min).** Geography: draw the one diagram and the one freehand India/world map for the topic, timed at under 2 minutes each. Society: write out the 3-5 data points with source and year. History and culture: a 6-10 point timeline or a comparison table.
4. **Timed answer (12-20 min).** One PYQ, 10-marker in about 7 minutes or 15-marker in about 11 minutes (the paper allows about 9 minutes per question on average), handwritten where possible. Working professionals on weeknights: one 10-marker only; on weekends: a 10 and a 15 back to back.
5. **Grade and fix (10 min).** Grade using the rubric below; have the learner rewrite only the weakest section (usually the conclusion or the missing example), not the whole answer.
6. **Tonight's close (2 min).** Write the one-page note; schedule a 5-minute recall of this topic on day 3 and day 10.
Depth guidance: a topic that has appeared twice or more since 2013 deserves a full 90-minute slot; a single-appearance topic gets 60 minutes; a never-asked syllabus corner gets a 20-minute skim with one framework.

### Grading a learner's answer
Apply the skill's common answer-writing rubric (structure, directive compliance, content accuracy, examples, presentation, word limit) and then run these GS I-specific checks:
- **Chronology:** dates and sequence correct; events placed in the right phase (e.g. Swarajists after NCM, Poona Pact after the Communal Award). A wrong date in a history answer costs more than a missing one; mark it explicitly.
- **Named examples:** at least one specific per point — person, act, monument, text, place, data figure. Generic statements ("many reforms were made", "many temples were built") are flagged as empty.
- **Diagram or map (geography):** present, labelled, relevant and drawn in under 2 minutes. If absent in a physical-geography or location question, say so first; it is usually the cheapest marks lost.
- **Regional balance:** freedom-struggle and society answers should not read as Bengal-Bombay-Delhi only; geography answers should name regions beyond the Himalaya-Ganga plain; culture answers should pair North and South examples when the question allows.
- **Analysis over narration:** for "why/extent/assess/critically" directives, check that each point carries a reason or a judgement, not just a fact; check that "critically" answers show both sides and reach a verdict.
- **Data hygiene (society):** every number has a source and a year; no figures the learner cannot source; latest release preferred, Census 2011 acceptable where nothing newer exists.
- **Introduction and conclusion:** the intro defines or dates; the conclusion answers the question as asked (not a generic "government should take steps"). For society topics, a constitutional, committee or judgment reference in the conclusion is a plus.
- **Word discipline:** about 150/250 words with headed sub-sections; over-length answers are penalised in feedback because they cost time on other questions in the real paper.
Feedback format: score out of 10 or 15 (an estimate against the rubric, not a prediction of the UPSC mark), the single biggest gap, the one thing to add (with the exact example or diagram), and one sentence on what was done well. Then set the next PYQ from the same topic line for the following session.
