# CSAT (GS Paper II) — Resource Map

> Scope note: this map is built from the 2011–2024 UPSC CSAT papers plus the standard books named below. Keep the papers set after 2024 untouched until the last month and sit them as full-length mocks; do not assume their pattern differs from what is described here unless you have checked the paper yourself.

## Weight and exam behaviour

- **Format**: 80 questions, 200 marks, 2.5 marks each, 2 hours, question paper set in Hindi and English. Negative marking: one-third of 2.5 = **0.833 per wrong answer**. Qualifying: **33% = 66.67 marks**, i.e. 27 net correct-equivalents (27 × 2.5 = 67.5). Marks are not counted for merit, but failing CSAT means GS Paper I is not evaluated.
- **Official syllabus heads** (unchanged since 2011): Comprehension; Interpersonal skills including communication skills; Logical reasoning and analytical ability; Decision-making and problem-solving; General mental ability; Basic numeracy (numbers and their relations, orders of magnitude, etc. — Class X level); Data interpretation (charts, graphs, tables, data sufficiency etc. — Class X level).
- **Three blocks in every paper since 2015 (the qualifying era)**: (a) reading comprehension (passages followed by inference/assumption/"crux" questions), (b) basic numeracy + general mental ability (Class X arithmetic, number properties, counting, sequences, sets), (c) logical/analytical reasoning + data interpretation (puzzles, arrangements, syllogism-type deductions, tables/graphs, data sufficiency). Decision-making questions (no negative marking, graded credit) appeared only in 2011–2013 and have not reappeared. The English Language Comprehension questions (set in English only) were excluded from grading in 2014 after the protests and dropped from 2015.
- **Trend (qualitatively, ~2017–2024)**: the paper has drifted from RC-heavy to **quant/reasoning-heavy**. Passages became shorter (often one passage → one question) and more abstract (philosophy, ethics, environment, governance), with "most logical and rational inference/assumption" stems that punish outside knowledge. Number-property questions (digits, remainders, divisibility, factorials), counting problems (handshakes, number of ways, counting figures), seating/arrangement puzzles, and clock/calendar questions grew. 2023 is widely regarded as the hardest CSAT; 2024 was felt to be a little easier but still math-heavy. Do not plan for an "easy" year.
- **Difficulty is uneven inside the paper**: every paper has a band of questions that are near-trivial for anyone who has practised PYQs, a middle band, and a band of time-sinks. Qualifying comes from securing the first two bands, not from attempting everything.
- **How negative marking should shape attempts**: one wrong answer wipes out a third of a right one. A sane target is ~45–50 attempts at ≥75% accuracy (e.g. 36 right, 12 wrong → 90 − 10 = 80). Three-pass strategy: pass 1 (≈40 min) every quant/reasoning question you can finish under 90 seconds; pass 2 (≈55 min) all passages; pass 3 the leftovers you have a method for. **Never guess in RC between two close options** unless the other two are eliminated; never guess a math question you did not actually solve.
- **Time per question is the real constraint**: 80 Qs in 120 min = 90 seconds each. A passage-question pair realistically costs 2.5–3 min, so quant/reasoning must be done at ~60 seconds for the easy ones.

## Mapping / data to memorise

CSAT has no "facts" in the GS sense; what must be memorised is a small set of numbers, rules and formulas so that no time is spent deriving them in the hall.

**Numbers**
- Multiplication tables to 20; squares 1–30; cubes 1–15; powers of 2 up to 2^12 (4096), of 3 up to 3^6 (729); factorials to 7! (5040) and the fact that 10! ends in two zeros.
- Primes below 100 (25 of them); 1 is neither prime nor composite; 2 is the only even prime.
- Divisibility rules for 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 (the 7 and 13 rules only if you have spare capacity).
- Unit-digit cycles: 2, 3, 7, 8 cycle every 4; 4 and 9 every 2; 0, 1, 5, 6 fixed.
- Number of zeros at the end of n! = ⌊n/5⌋ + ⌊n/25⌋ + ⌊n/125⌋ + …
- Number of factors of N = (a+1)(b+1)… for N = p^a q^b…; sum of first n naturals n(n+1)/2; first n odd numbers = n²; first n squares n(n+1)(2n+1)/6; first n cubes [n(n+1)/2]².
- Fraction ↔ percent ↔ decimal table: 1/2…1/12, 1/15, 1/16, 1/20, 1/25, and 2/3, 3/4, 3/8, 5/8, 7/8.
- Pythagorean triples: (3,4,5), (5,12,13), (8,15,17), (7,24,25), (9,40,41) and their multiples.
- Alphabet positions A=1 … Z=26 and reverse (Z=1); the mnemonic "EJOTY" (5, 10, 15, 20, 25).

**Rules & formulas**
- Percentage: successive change a + b + ab/100; "x% more ⇒ 100x/(100+x) % less".
- Average speed over equal distances 2xy/(x+y); km/h × 5/18 = m/s; relative speed = sum (opposite directions) / difference (same direction); train crossing a platform uses the sum of lengths.
- Time & work by LCM; efficiency inversely proportional to days; wages in proportion to work done.
- Boats: speed of boat (D+U)/2, stream (D−U)/2.
- Mixture replacement: final = initial × (1 − x/V)^n; alligation cross.
- SI = PRT/100; CI amount = P(1 + R/100)^T; CI − SI for 2 years = P(R/100)².
- Clock: angle = abs(30H − 5.5M); hands coincide 11 times in 12 h, are at right angles 22 times in 12 h, opposite (straight line, 180°) 11 times in 12 h; minute hand gains 55 minute-spaces on the hour hand per hour.
- Calendar: odd days — ordinary year 1, leap year 2, 100 years 5, 200 years 3, 300 years 1, 400 years 0; a century year is leap only if divisible by 400; the calendar of a year repeats after 6, 11 or 28 years depending on leap-year position.
- Sets: n(A∪B) = n(A)+n(B)−n(A∩B); three-set formula n(A∪B∪C) = n(A)+n(B)+n(C) − n(A∩B) − n(B∩C) − n(A∩C) + n(A∩B∩C); "exactly two" = sum of pairwise intersections − 3×triple; "neither" = total − union.
- Counting: handshakes/matches in a round robin n(n−1)/2; diagonals n(n−3)/2; squares in an n×n grid = 1²+2²+…+n²; rectangles in an n×n grid = [n(n+1)/2]²; ways to choose r of n = nCr; arrangements of n items with k identical = n!/k!.
- Painted cube cut into n³ pieces: 3 faces 8; 2 faces 12(n−2); 1 face 6(n−2)²; 0 faces (n−2)³.
- Dice: a face is never opposite any face it is seen together with in a view; once a face's four neighbours are known from the views, the remaining face is its opposite. Opposite faces sum to 7 only if the question says it is a standard die.
- Syllogism conversions: "All A are B" → "Some B are A" (valid) but not "All B are A"; "No A is B" ↔ "No B is A"; "Some A are B" ↔ "Some B are A"; "Some A are not B" has no valid conversion.
- Implication: "If P then Q" is equivalent only to its contrapositive "If not Q then not P"; the converse ("If Q then P") and inverse ("If not P then not Q") do not follow. "P only if Q" means P → Q (Q is necessary for P), whereas "P if Q" means Q → P.
- Mensuration: area/perimeter of square, rectangle, triangle (½bh and Heron's formula), circle (πr², 2πr), parallelogram, trapezium; volume & surface area of cube, cuboid, cylinder, cone, sphere, hemisphere; π ≈ 22/7 or 3.14 as the options dictate.
- AP: Tn = a + (n−1)d; Sn = n/2 [2a + (n−1)d] = n/2 (first + last). GP: Tn = ar^(n−1).
- Probability: P(at least one) = 1 − P(none); total outcomes for two dice 36, for three coins 8; a pack has 52 cards, 4 suits, 13 ranks, 12 face cards.

**RC definitions (memorise verbatim)**
- Inference: must follow from the passage, nothing more, nothing less.
- Assumption: unstated premise without which the author's argument collapses.
- Crux/central idea: the single statement the whole passage is written to support.
- Red-flag words in options: always, never, only, all, none, must, completely, solely, cannot — accept them only if the passage itself uses an equivalent absolute.

## How to teach a topic from this subject

When the learner says "teach me X" (e.g., "teach me clocks", "explain painted cube", "teach me inference questions"), follow this order, in this length (aim for one screen of text, then practise):

1. **Define the question type in UPSC's words** (1–2 lines): what the question looks like in the paper, how often it appears (qualitatively: "most years" / "some years" / "rare"), and whether it belongs to pass 1 (60-second) or pass 3 (leave-for-last).
2. **Minimal theory** (3–6 lines): the one or two rules needed at Class X level. No bank-exam trick inventory. For RC types: the definition (inference/assumption/crux) and the elimination test.
3. **Worked PYQ** (one real UPSC question or a faithful paraphrase): show the solving path in the fewest steps, stating where a learner would waste time and the moment the answer becomes certain. For RC: show each option and the one-phrase reason it survives or dies.
4. **Key facts / formula table**: the rows from "Mapping / data to memorise" that this topic uses, nothing else.
5. **What UPSC has asked** (3–5 bullets): the recurring variants of this type across 2011–2024 (e.g., for calendar: day-of-week from a known anchor; same-calendar years; day count between two dates).
6. **Three traps**: specific wrong turns, each tied to how the options are built (e.g., "the option equal to the one-face count is always present when the two-face count is asked").
7. **Two check questions in UPSC format**: four options each, statement-style where the type allows ("Consider the following statements… Which of the statements given above is/are correct?"), one easy and one at paper level. Give the answer and a one-line reason only after the learner answers; if they get the second wrong, assign 10 PYQs of this type from the topic-wise compilation as the next night's drill.
8. **Scheduling line**: tell the learner where this topic sits in their plan ("1 night, then 20 PYQ drills over the next 2 weeks") and what to write on their formula card.

For RC specifically, replace step 3 with: paste a short UPSC-style passage; ask the learner to answer first; then walk through the options; then show the "mildest option" and "outside knowledge" patterns explicitly.

## Revision & test strategy

- **Load and placement**: the plan below assumes CSAT gets the full weeknight block for ~10–12 weeks (roughly 150 hours). When GS is running in parallel (the normal case), give CSAT 30 min of RC every weeknight plus one 3-hour weekend unit (~5.5 h/week) and spread the units over ~20 weeks; the last 6–8 weeks before Prelims carry the full-length tests either way.
- **Revisions**: three structured passes. Pass 1 (weeks 1–8): learn each unit through PYQs-first, book-second. Pass 2 (weeks 9–12): re-solve every PYQ you got wrong or slow, from the error log, blind. Pass 3 (final 3 weeks): formula cards + the last 5 years' papers re-timed.
- **Short notes** should fit on 6–8 pages total: (1) formula/fact card (numbers, rules, mensuration, clock/calendar, sets, counting, painted cube), (2) RC option-killer list with PYQ examples, (3) syllogism/implication rule card, (4) blood-relation and direction conventions, (5) error log (topic, year/Q, cause), (6) per-test attempt/accuracy table. No theory paragraphs.
- **PYQ years**: do 2015–2024 timed, section by section, as the backbone (these are the qualifying-era papers). Use 2011–2014 for RC volume only; their long multi-question passages train stamina, but weight their question stems less since the style has shifted. Keep the two most recent papers untouched until the last month and sit them as full mocks.
- **Sectional tests**: start in week 3 — one 30-minute sectional per weekend (alternate quant/reasoning and RC). From week 9, one full-length 2-hour test every Saturday, review on Sunday with the error log; stop adding new topics after week 10.
- **The nightly 2-hour block** for a working professional: 30 min RC (3 passages, timed), 45 min one quant/reasoning unit (PYQs first, then book exercise to fix errors), 30 min re-solving yesterday's errors, 15 min formula card. Weekends: Saturday 3-hour unit block (new topic) + Sunday 2–3 hours (test/review).
- **Attempt discipline to rehearse in every test**: three passes; mark every question as Solved / Method-known / Skip within 10 seconds of reading; never exceed 3 minutes on any question; keep a running count of confident attempts — qualifying is a function of 30+ sure answers with few wrong ones, not of 80 attempts.

## Linking current affairs to this subject

CSAT does not test current affairs as facts, but editorials and reports are the raw material of its passages and data sets. A news item becomes a CSAT question in one of three ways: (1) an editorial paragraph becomes an inference/assumption passage, (2) a published table or chart becomes a DI set, (3) a reported figure becomes a percentage/ratio arithmetic question. The learner's job is not to remember the news but to recognise the register and to practise the conversion.

Five example conversions (static-safe, pre-2025):

1. **Editorial on climate finance and developing countries' obligations (any year 2015–2024)** → *RC inference*: a 150-word paragraph arguing that mitigation burdens should be differentiated by historical emissions; question: "Which of the following is the most logical and rational inference from the passage? (a) Developed countries must pay all costs (b) Historical responsibility is a relevant basis for differentiated obligations (c) Developing countries need not act until compensated (d) Climate finance is unnecessary." Teaching point: (b) is the mildest option; (a), (c), (d) use absolutes.
2. **Census 2011 / decadal growth reporting** → *percentage arithmetic*: "The population of a town increased by 20% in a decade and then decreased by 20% in the next. The net change is: (a) 0% (b) 4% decrease (c) 4% increase (d) 2% decrease." Teaching point: successive percentage change, not additive (1.2 × 0.8 = 0.96 → (b)).
3. **Economic Survey-style sectoral share chart (agriculture/industry/services as % of GVA)** → *pie-chart DI*: given shares and a total, ask for the absolute value of one sector, the angle it subtends, and the ratio between two sectors. Teaching point: 1% = 3.6°, and compare shares without computing absolutes when the total cancels.
4. **Report on a vaccination or literacy drive giving "x% of eligible population covered, of which y% women"** → *set/Venn or ratio question*: "In a district 60% of the eligible population was covered; of those covered, 55% were women; if the eligible population has equal men and women, what fraction of eligible men were covered?" Teaching point: percent-of-percent and a 2×2 table instead of a Venn (men covered = 27% of eligible, out of 50% → 54% of men).
5. **Editorial on universal basic income versus targeted subsidies (debated widely from the Economic Survey 2016–17 onward)** → *RC assumption*: passage argues that targeting leaks and UBI is simpler; question: "Which of the following assumptions is/are implicit in the passage? 1. Administrative simplicity reduces leakage. 2. The state can afford a universal transfer. Select: (a) 1 only (b) 2 only (c) Both 1 and 2 (d) Neither 1 nor 2." Teaching point: an assumption must be *necessary* for the argument; test each by negating it and seeing whether the argument collapses.

General rule to teach: when a learner brings a news item, convert it on the spot — write a 120-word neutral paragraph from it and build one inference question with four options (one mild/correct, one absolute, one outside-knowledge, one scope-shifted); or extract any two numbers from it and build a percentage/ratio question. This is a practice technique, not a prediction technique: the paper's passages will be different, but the option patterns will not.
