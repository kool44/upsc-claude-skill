# GS Paper III — Resource Map (Mains)

Scope: Technology, Economic Development, Bio-diversity, Environment, Security and Disaster Management. Typically 20 questions / 250 marks / 3 hours: 10 questions of 10 marks (about 150 words) and 10 of 15 marks (about 250 words), all compulsory; the split has held since 2018 but confirm the current pattern on upsc.gov.in. This file assumes the learner has read the Prelims maps (`economy.md`, `environment.md`, `science-tech.md`, `geography.md`) and does not repeat chapter-level Prelims content. GS III rewards a different muscle from GS I/II: a specific number, a named scheme or committee, a diagram, and a way forward that an administrator could act on tomorrow.

## Answer frameworks that work in GS III

Each skeleton is for a 250-word answer; for 150 words drop one middle block and halve the rest. Always open with a definition or a number; always close with a forward-looking line.

1. **Status -> Issues -> Government steps -> Gaps -> Way forward** (economy, agriculture, industry, infrastructure)
   - Intro (30 words): define + 1 Survey number.
   - Status (40): where India stands, 2 data points.
   - Issues (60): 3-4 bullets, each a cause with a consequence.
   - Government steps (50): 3 named schemes/laws with ministry, one line each.
   - Gaps (30): why the steps fall short (implementation, finance, federal, behavioural).
   - Way forward (40): 3 concrete, named recommendations (a committee's, a state's model, a technology).
   - Worked example — "constraints in marketing of agricultural produce": status (share of produce sold through regulated markets; number of mandis against the roughly 5 km access norm suggested by the National Commission on Farmers); issues (fragmented markets, intermediaries, no grading/assaying, cascading cess, inter-state barriers, weak cold chain); steps (e-NAM 2016, Model APLM Act 2017, Agriculture Infrastructure Fund 2020, 10,000 FPO scheme, Operation Greens); gaps (e-NAM trade mostly intra-mandi, uneven state adoption of model Acts, FPO viability); way forward (Dalwai's integrated value-chain approach, assaying infrastructure, warehouse receipts/eNWR, private markets, a uniform market fee).

2. **Concept -> Rationale -> Provisions -> Assessment (for/against) -> Balanced conclusion** (budgeting, a law, a policy: GST, FRBM, Labour Codes, NFSA, draft EIA 2020)
   - Define in one line, give the problem it was meant to solve, 4-5 key provisions, then two columns (achievements vs criticisms, each with evidence), then a conclusion that names what would make it work.
   - Worked example — "Labour Codes": concept (29 central laws consolidated into 4 codes); rationale (compliance burden, formalisation, rigidity under the Industrial Disputes Act's 100-worker threshold); provisions (universal minimum wage and a floor wage; retrenchment-approval threshold raised to 300 workers; fixed-term employment; social security for gig and platform workers; single licence/registration; OSH cover for inter-state migrants); for (ease of doing business, formalisation, gig coverage); against (easier hire-and-fire, strike-notice conditions, weak inspection via the "inspector-cum-facilitator", rules not yet notified by all states as of 2024); conclusion (phased notification with tripartite consultation; track formal-job outcomes through PLFS).

3. **Technology -> How it works (2-3 lines) -> Applications -> India's status -> Challenges -> Way forward** (S&T)
   - Worked example — "AI in clinical diagnosis": define AI/ML; how it works (pattern learning from labelled images/records); applications (radiology triage, diabetic retinopathy screening, TB detection from X-rays, drug discovery, predictive analytics); India's status (IndiaAI Mission 2024, NITI Aayog's National Strategy for AI 2018 "AI for All", ICMR ethical guidelines for AI in biomedical research and healthcare 2023, ABDM health records); challenges (data bias, consent, privacy under the DPDP Act 2023, liability, digital divide); way forward (anonymisation, algorithmic audits, human-in-the-loop, regulatory sandbox).

4. **Hazard profile -> Vulnerability -> Institutional framework -> Case study -> Reforms** (disaster management)
   - Hazard profile: mechanism in 2-3 lines plus India's exposure (NDMA's percent of area/population).
   - Vulnerability: physical, social, economic, environmental (the 2019 PYQ's types).
   - Institutional framework: DM Act 2005 chain (NDMA-SDMA-DDMA), the relevant NDMA guideline, NDRF, the early-warning agency (IMD/INCOIS/CWC/GSI), the Sendai priority it maps to.
   - Case study: one named Indian event with what went wrong/right.
   - Reforms: prevention-mitigation-preparedness measures, named.
   - Worked example — "urban floods": profile (short-duration high-intensity rain, impervious surfaces, encroached drains; NDMA Urban Flooding guidelines 2010); vulnerability (slums on floodplains, informal economy, hospitals in low-lying areas); framework (ULB as nodal agency per the guidelines, IMD nowcasting, AMRUT 2.0 drainage); cases (Chennai 2015 — reservoir release plus the encroached Pallikaranai marsh; Mumbai 2005 — Mithi river; Bengaluru 2022 — lake encroachment); reforms (storm-water drain master plans, sponge-city features, wetland restoration, flood zoning in master plans, real-time reservoir rule curves, insurance).
   - Diagram: the DM cycle (prevention -> mitigation -> preparedness -> response -> recovery) with one measure per phase.

5. **Threat -> Drivers -> State response -> Gaps -> Reforms** (internal security)
   - Worked example — "LWE": threat (armed Maoist insurgency centred on the Dandakaranya/Bastar belt, the "Red Corridor"; trend numbers from the latest MHA report); drivers (land alienation, displacement by mining, weak implementation of PESA/FRA/Fifth Schedule, absence of the state, forest dependence, Maoist ideology and extortion economy); response (SAMADHAN doctrine 2017, Security Related Expenditure scheme, Special Central Assistance for most-affected districts, Road Requirement Plan, Aspirational Districts Programme, CoBRA, Bastariya Battalion, surrender-cum-rehabilitation, mobile towers); gaps (civil administration lagging security gains, forest-rights backlog, police vacancies, inter-state coordination); reforms (2nd ARC's *Public Order* report on the governance deficit, the Planning Commission Expert Group (2008) on development challenges in extremist-affected areas, tribal-sensitive development, special courts, Eklavya schools).
   - Map sketch: the affected states with the Bastar core, or the three borders for a border question.

6. **Problem -> Causes (3 lenses: natural, anthropogenic, governance) -> Impacts (health, economy, ecology) -> Legal/institutional response -> Gaps -> Way forward** (pollution, degradation, climate)
   - Worked example — "solid waste": problem (urban India's daily generation and low processing share — update from CPCB's latest annual report); causes (consumption growth, no segregation, informal recycling, ULB finances); impacts (methane from dumpsites, leachate, Deonar/Ghazipur fires, health of waste-pickers); response (Solid Waste Management Rules 2016, Plastic Waste Management Rules 2016 and the single-use plastic ban of July 2022, E-Waste (Management) Rules 2022, Swachh Bharat Mission-Urban 2.0 "garbage-free cities", waste-to-energy, EPR); gaps (source segregation, legacy dumps, bio-mining pace); way forward (decentralised composting, circular-economy roadmap, the Indore model, EPR enforcement).

7. **Value chain: Upstream -> Midstream -> Downstream, with constraints at each link** (food processing, agri-marketing, supply chain, logistics, MSME)
   - Draw a horizontal chain and write 2 constraints and 1 intervention under each node. Worked example — food processing: upstream (fragmented holdings, no grading, seasonal gluts; FPOs, Model Contract Farming Act 2018); midstream (low processing share, cold-chain gaps, SME finance; PMKSY cold chain, PMFME, PLI); downstream (multi-brand retail restrictions, export standards, branding; 100% FDI under the approval route in retail of food products manufactured in India, APEDA, GI tags).

8. **Then vs Now comparison table + evaluation** (NITI Aayog vs Planning Commission; EIA 2006 vs draft 2020; Hyogo vs Sendai; GDP series 2004-05 vs 2011-12; MSP on A2+FL vs C2; reactive vs proactive DM)
   - A 4-5 row table on criteria (purpose, structure, process, coverage, accountability), then a paragraph on what the change achieved and what it lost, then a conclusion. Examiners reward the table when the question literally asks "how is X different from Y".

Cross-cutting devices: (a) a flowchart for any process question (EIA steps; GST Council -> compensation; money-laundering placement-layering-integration); (b) a 2x2 for "pros and cons" questions; (c) one state model as evidence (Sikkim organic, Odisha cyclone evacuation, Indore waste, Telangana Rythu Bandhu, Andhra Pradesh community-managed natural farming, Kerala Kudumbashree); (d) one committee per topic; (e) one SDG number in the conclusion.

### Landmark disasters and what changed after each
- **Latur 1993 (earthquake)** and **Odisha Super Cyclone 1999 (October; around 10,000 deaths)**: the High Powered Committee on DM was set up in August 1999 and its remit widened after the cyclone and Bhuj; Odisha State Disaster Management Authority (1999), cyclone shelters, and the policy shift from relief to preparedness.
- **Bhuj/Gujarat 2001 (26 January, Mw 7.7; about 13,800 official deaths, higher by some estimates)**: Gujarat set up GSDMA (2001) and passed the first state DM Act (2003); owner-driven reconstruction; earthquake-resistant building codes; momentum for the national Act.
- **Indian Ocean tsunami 2004 (26 December; India's deaths above 10,000, mostly Tamil Nadu and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands)**: Disaster Management Act 2005; NDMA (2005-06); Indian Tsunami Early Warning Centre at INCOIS, Hyderabad (2007); coastal bio-shields and mangrove emphasis; NDMA Tsunami guidelines 2010.
- **Mumbai floods 2005 (944 mm in a day)**: NDMA Urban Flooding guidelines 2010; Mithi river works; urban drainage master plans; ULBs made nodal.
- **Uttarakhand 2013 (Kedarnath, June; thousands dead or missing)**: Supreme Court halted new hydro projects in the Alaknanda-Bhagirathi basins pending expert review (Ravi Chopra committee); pilgrim regulation; emphasis on Himalayan early warning; the 2016 cloudburst PYQ drew on it.

## Teaching and grading in this paper

### Teaching a GS III topic in a 60-90 minute slot (weeknight)

Use this sequence in the chat; the learner should finish the slot with one written answer and one page of reusable material.

1. **Frame (5 min)**: state the syllabus line, its weight, and the 3-5 PYQ themes from the table above. Name the single primary source and the exact chapter/section.
2. **Teach the content (20-25 min)**: deliver it here in the order the answer will use — definition, status with 3-4 data points (flag each as "update from the latest Survey"), causes/issues, government response (scheme + ministry + year), gaps, way forward (committee + state model). Add one diagram the learner can reproduce in 60 seconds (value chain, DM cycle, border map, EIA flow).
3. **Build the framework (10 min)**: pick the matching skeleton from the frameworks section; fill it with the content just taught; show the 250-word budget per block.
4. **Timed writing (15 min for 250 words / 8 min for 150)**: give one PYQ, paraphrased with the year; the learner writes under the clock.
5. **Grade (15 min)**: apply the checklist below; quote their text where marks were lost; rewrite their weakest block in 60-80 words.
6. **Consolidate (5-10 min)**: a 10-line card — 5 numbers, 3 schemes with ministry, 1 committee, 1 case study, 1 diagram name. This card is the revision unit.

Weeknight with 2 hours: two slots (one economy/agriculture, one from S&T/environment/disaster/security) or one slot plus 30 minutes of Survey chapter reading. Weekend day with 7 hours: morning — one Survey chapter plus the matching Ramesh Singh/Shankar section (2.5 h); afternoon — a sectional test of 10 GS III questions in 90 minutes, then 60 minutes of self-grading with this file; evening — 1.5 h of cards and PYQ mapping. A full-timer runs the same weekend shape on four or five days a week and adds a second sectional test. Between Prelims and Mains (typically about 100 days), GS III needs roughly 3-4 full-paper mocks (20 questions, 3 hours) spaced about two weeks apart; a working professional should book leave for the last 4-6 weeks before Mains.

"What to do tonight" default: if the learner has read nothing on the topic, teach steps 1-3 and set the timed answer for the next night; if they have read it, go straight to step 4 and spend the saved time on a second question. ### GS III-specific grading checks

Score on the general rubric in `answer-writing.md` (structure, content, presentation, word limit, time), then apply these paper-specific checks; each failed check should be named in the feedback with the fix.

1. **Data cited** — at least two figures with a source (Survey year, PLFS, MHA report, ISFR) in a 250-word answer; one in 150 words. A figure without a source or with a stale year is flagged; an answer with no number in an economy/agriculture/environment question loses the "content" band.
2. **Scheme, law or committee named** — at least one scheme with its ministry and launch year, or one Act with its year, or one committee with its recommendation. "Government has launched many schemes" without a name is a deduction.
3. **Diagram or flowchart present where the question is a process, a system or a comparison** — EIA steps, DM cycle, value chain, money-laundering stages, border map, "then vs now" table. A relevant diagram that saves words earns presentation marks; a decorative diagram does not.
4. **Way forward is concrete** — each recommendation should name who does what (a committee's recommendation, a state model, a technology, a legal change), not "awareness should be increased" or "government should take steps". Three concrete points beat six generic ones.
5. **Directive obeyed** — "critically examine"/"do you agree" needs both sides and a stated position; "elaborate"/"discuss" needs breadth; "comment" needs an assessment, not a description; a comparison question needs a table.
6. **Keywords from the syllabus line and the stem reused** — "inclusive", "upstream/downstream", "vulnerability", "indigenisation", "non-state actors", "wise use": the examiner scans for them.
7. **Current-year hook** — one reference to the latest Budget, Survey, COP, MHA report, mission launch or incident (pre-dating the exam) shows currency; more than two makes the answer a news summary.
8. **Security and disaster answers must be institutionally precise** — the right agency (CERT-In vs NCIIPC vs I4C; NDMA vs NDRF vs SDMA), the right Act and section, the right force for the border. Wrong agency attribution is penalised as a factual error.
9. **S&T answers must contain the mechanism in plain words** (2-3 lines) before the applications; an answer that is all policy and no science is marked incomplete, and vice versa.
10. **Conclusion** — one sentence that links to a goal with a date (SDG 2030, net zero 2070, Viksit Bharat 2047, Sendai 2030) or quotes a committee; never a restatement of the question.

Feedback format for the learner: score out of 10 or 15 with a band (this file's bands for a 15-marker: below 4 / 4-6 / 6-8 / above 8, scaled proportionally for 10 marks; these are the mentor's bands, not UPSC's, which publishes no marks distribution), three lines quoting what cost marks, one rewritten block, and one data card to memorise. Repeat the same question after seven days; a second attempt should show the checklist fully met in the same time.
