# The Optional Subject — Choosing and Preparing

Scope of this file: how to choose an optional, how the optional papers work, how to prepare one on a working professional's hours, starter maps for the six most-chosen subjects, and how to grade optional answers. Prelims-level chapter maps live in the subject files; GS/Essay frameworks live in `gs1.md`…`essay.md`. Verify any year-specific detail (form dates, exact question split in a given year, optional-wise candidate counts) against the notification, the previous papers and the annual report at upsc.gov.in. Nothing in this file relies on a published UPSC marks distribution by optional; UPSC does not publish one.

## Why the optional matters and how to choose

**Weight.** Two papers, 250 marks each = **500 of the 1750 Mains marks** (about 29%) — more than Essay (250) and more than any single GS paper. Essay and the four GS papers are, by common report, marked in a fairly narrow band for most candidates; the optional is where the same candidate can swing by a large margin between a well-chosen, well-practised subject and a badly chosen one. Treat the optional as the largest single source of variance in the Mains total that is under your control.

**Declared once.** The optional (and the medium of answering, and the Paper A language) is entered in the online application form — the notification typically appears in **January–February** at upsc.gov.in. Once the form (and any correction window UPSC announces for that year) closes, UPSC does not allow a change for that year's exam. You can change it in a later attempt, but that means restarting ~400–600 hours of reading. Decide before the form, not after Prelims.

**Selection criteria, in this order:**

1. **Sustained interest.** You will spend 400–600 hours reading, writing and revising it, most of it alone, much of it after Prelims when you are tired. The question is not "do I like this?" but "will I still open this book in week 30?" Dislike of a subject is the most common reason an optional is abandoned mid-year.
2. **Overlap with GS and Essay.** PSIR, PubAd, Sociology, Geography, History, Anthropology and Economics each feed one or more GS papers and give you a thinker-vocabulary for Essay and GS IV. Overlap saves hours; it does not make the optional easier, because the optional is examined at a depth GS never reaches.
3. **Syllabus length.** Read the syllabus (upsc.gov.in → Examinations → Syllabus → Civil Services Examination) and count topics honestly. Anthropology and Philosophy are shorter; History, Geography, PSIR, Law and Medical Science are long. Short syllabus = more revision rounds within the same hours.
4. **Sources and evaluation.** A standard book set, 2013–2024 papers you can map, and somebody (a mentor, a test series, or this chat) who can grade your answers against the subject's expectations. A subject with no evaluator is a subject in which you will not know your standard until the result.
5. **Scoring consistency.** Qualitative, year-to-year: some optionals have given stable scores to most who prepared them well; others have swung with the examiner panel. Treat any "average marks by optional" number you see online as unverified; UPSC publishes optional-wise *candidate counts* in its annual report, not averages.
6. **Your background and medium.** A graduate of the subject starts ahead — perhaps by 100–150 hours — but only if the graduate syllabus matched UPSC's. Regional-medium candidates need the standard sources to exist in their language (Hindi has the most; other languages vary) or must be ready to read in English and write in their medium.

**Decision checklist** (answer all eight before you declare):

- [ ] I have read the full UPSC syllabus of both papers of this optional, not a coaching summary.
- [ ] I have opened two actual UPSC papers (2023 and 2024) and understood what is being asked in at least half the questions.
- [ ] I can name the 3–5 standard books and I have access to them (bought or library), in my medium.
- [ ] I have a way to get answers evaluated (mentor / test series / this chat with the checklist in the last section).
- [ ] I can give it ~9 hours a week for ~45 weeks, or the equivalent, without starving GS.
- [ ] I have completed the 2-week trial below and preferred this subject to the other shortlisted one.
- [ ] I am not choosing it because a topper or a friend chose it.
- [ ] I have checked that my degree's advantage is real (compare the two syllabi) and not assumed.

**The 2-week trial method** (do this before the form opens; it costs ~20 hours):

- Week 1: shortlist two optionals using the criteria above. For each, pick one mid-syllabus topic (not the introductory chapter — e.g., Anthropology: *kinship and descent*; Sociology: *Weber's social action and ideal types*; Geography: *slope development*; PSIR: *Rawls and his communitarian critics*; PubAd: *Simon's decision-making*). Read that topic from the primary source listed in this file (2–3 hours per subject). Note how the reading felt at hour two.
- Week 2: from the 2013–2024 papers at upsc.gov.in, take one 15/20-mark question on that topic from each subject and write a full answer (250 words, 20–25 minutes, timed). Send both answers here for grading with the *Evaluating optional answers* checklist, or compare them yourself against the checklist.
- Decide on: which reading you wanted to continue; which answer came more naturally; which subject's papers you could imagine attempting 5 of 8. Tie-break on syllabus length and sources, not on hearsay.

**Tonight (if the form is weeks away):** download the syllabus PDF and the 2024 Paper I and II of your two shortlisted optionals from upsc.gov.in; read the syllabus of both end to end (40 minutes each). Tomorrow starts week 1 of the trial.

## Background-based suggestions

**Engineering / CS graduates (the largest group).**
- *Anthropology*: short syllabus, biological anthropology and genetics are science-flavoured, diagrams score; most engineers find Paper I readable. Risk: Paper II (Indian tribes, social structure) needs a humanities writing style some engineers never develop; sociocultural theory questions punish rote.
- *Mathematics*: only if you scored well in college mathematics and can give 600+ hours; marks are objective and consistent. Risk: the syllabus is two full undergraduate papers with zero GS overlap; an unfinished Mathematics syllabus is the worst outcome of any optional (you cannot bluff a 20-mark question you have not studied).
- *Geography* or *PSIR* for GS overlap: Geography if you like diagrams and maps, PSIR if you read politics and foreign affairs for pleasure. Risk: both are long; Geography's scores are reported as variable; PSIR needs continuous current-IR updating.

**Medical graduates.**
- *Medical Science*: your degree, consistent scores by report, fastest first reading. Risk: long syllabus and clinical detail fade quickly after internship; needs disciplined revision; near-zero GS overlap.
- *Anthropology*: genetics, evolution, primatology and human growth sit comfortably with your training; much shorter than Medical Science. Risk: the sociocultural half of Paper I and all of Paper II are new.
- *Psychology* if you did well in behavioural sciences. Risk: Paper II application questions need Indian examples and a social-science register.

**Commerce / CA / CS.**
- *Commerce & Accountancy*: numerical accuracy and taxation knowledge convert directly; consistent by report for prepared candidates. Risk: Paper II (OB, HRM, industrial relations) is a different subject from accounting and is routinely under-prepared; accounting-standard and tax references must be current.
- *Management* if your degree was MBA. Risk: few takers, thin evaluation ecosystem.
- *Sociology* or *PubAd* if you want GS overlap and a reading-light humanities subject. Risk: a humanities answer style must be built from zero.

**Law graduates.**
- *Law*: constitutional and administrative law double as GS II preparation; case-citation precision scores. Risk: long syllabus (international law, crimes, torts, contracts and mercantile law, contemporary legal developments); keeping statutes and recent judgments current.
- *PSIR*: political theory overlaps with jurisprudence; IR adds breadth. Risk: long; thinkers must be read in their own terms, not as legal doctrine.
- *Philosophy* for those who liked jurisprudence's abstract side. Risk: no GS I–III overlap.

**Humanities graduates (BA in history, political science, sociology, economics, geography).**
- *Your own subject*, after checking that the college syllabus matched UPSC's. Sociology, PSIR, Geography and History all have mature source sets. Risk: overconfidence — a BA reading is not a UPSC reading; PYQ-mapped answer writing still needs ~200 hours.
- *Anthropology* or *Sociology* if your subject's UPSC syllabus is long (History, Geography) and your hours are limited. Risk: giving up a subject you already partly know for one you must start from zero.
- *Economics* only for economics graduates comfortable with diagrams and models.

**Agriculture / veterinary / allied-science graduates.**
- *Agriculture*: direct degree conversion; consistent by report for well-practised candidates; GS III overlap on food security and agri-policy. Risk: Paper II (genetics, plant breeding, physiology, horticulture, plant protection) is wide and data-heavy.
- *Anthropology* as the short alternative; *Geography* for those from soil-science or agro-climatology backgrounds. Risk: as above for each.

**Working professionals with little time (≤ 24 h/week total).**
- *Anthropology*, *Sociology* or *Philosophy*: shortest syllabi, roughly 250–300 hours to first reading, fit into ~9 h/week across 45 weeks. Sociology and Anthropology also feed GS I and Essay, so the hours are not isolated. Risk: short syllabi are also well-known to examiners; generic answers are punished; you must still write 60–80 practice answers.
- *PubAd* if you are in government service and already think in these categories. Risk: variable scoring history by report; needs ARC reports read, not just summarised.
- Avoid Mathematics, Medical Science, Law, History and Geography on these hours unless your degree gives you a verified head start (compare the two syllabi) of 150 hours or more.

**Regional-language-medium candidates.**
- *Literature of your language*: you read the texts natively, the cohort is small, and scores for strong readers are reported as consistent; Paper A becomes easy. Risk: Paper I (history of the language and literature, linguistics) is academic and needs the standard literary history, not school memory; no GS overlap at all.
- *Sociology*, *PubAd*, *History* or *Geography* in Hindi medium: standard sources exist in Hindi (NCERT, IGNOU, Hindi editions of Laxmikanth, Majid Husain, Bipan Chandra). Risk: for most non-Hindi regional media, English sources must be read and translated in your head; budget ~20% more hours and practise translating technical terms consistently.
- *Anthropology* in Hindi has fewer standard texts than Sociology; check availability before choosing.

## How to prepare any optional

**The paper pattern (typically; verify with the actual 2023–2024 papers at upsc.gov.in).** Each optional paper: 250 marks, 3 hours, two sections (A and B) of four questions each — eight questions in all. **Q1 (Section A) and Q5 (Section B) are compulsory**, and typically consist of five short-note parts of 10 marks each (~150 words each) drawn from across the section. Of the remaining six questions you attempt **three**, choosing at least one from each section; each is typically split into parts such as 20 + 15 + 15 marks (~300/250/250 words), though the split varies by year and subject. Five questions × 50 marks = 250. Mathematics follows the same 8-question/5-attempt frame but the parts are problems, not notes; there is no word limit and a complete solution with working is what scores. Geography Paper II and History Paper I typically open with a compulsory map question in place of short notes (see the Geography map and the History row above). Writing time per mark is about 0.7 minutes: a 10-mark note gets ~7 minutes, a 20-mark part ~14 minutes. Plan to use the whole 3 hours; a common and avoidable loss is leaving one or two Q1/Q5 parts blank.

**Where the previous papers are.** upsc.gov.in → Examinations → Previous Question Papers → Civil Services (Main) Examination → year → "<Optional> Paper I / Paper II". Download 2013–2024 (24 papers) on day one. Print or tabulate them; you will refer to them every week.

**Phases (for ~45 weeks at ~9 h/week ≈ 400 h; scale proportionally):**

1. **Syllabus mapping (week 1, ~6 h).** Copy the UPSC syllabus into a sheet, one row per sub-topic (depending on how finely you split, roughly 70–110 rows for a humanities optional; a few dozen problem types for Mathematics). Columns: primary source + chapter, PYQ years, status (read / notes / answers written / revised ×N).
2. **PYQ mapping 2013–2024 (weeks 1–2, ~8 h).** Go through all 24 papers and write every question's year against its syllabus row. You will typically find that a minority of sub-topics attract the majority of questions. Mark those as Tier 1. This map, not the book's table of contents, decides your reading order.
3. **Standard source reading, once (weeks 2–22, ~180 h).** One primary source per topic, read in the PYQ-weighted order in the starter maps below, with the PYQs of that topic open beside you. After each chapter, write the questions UPSC asked on it from memory, in bullet form. Do not buy a second book for the same topic until the first is finished.
4. **Notes (continuous, part of phase 3 — not a separate pass).** One A4 side per sub-topic: definition, 3–5 thinkers/models with their one-line position, 2 examples or case studies (Indian where possible), one diagram if the subject uses them, and the list of PYQs with their year. These are what you revise; the book is not reopened after week 30.
5. **Answer writing with the paper pattern (weeks 10–40, ~90 h).** From week 10, two answers a week on topics already read: one 10-mark note (150 words, 7 minutes) and one 20-mark part (300 words, 14 minutes), timed, by hand. Send them for grading (this chat, with the checklist in the last section). From week 24, one full section (Q1 + two questions, 90 minutes) every fortnight. Aim for 60–80 graded answers before the test series.
6. **Test series (weeks 30–44, ~50 h).** 6–8 full-length papers, timed, each followed by a 2-hour rework of the questions that lost marks. If no paid series is affordable, construct papers from 2013–2016 PYQs (one Section A + one Section B from different years) and grade against the checklist.
7. **Revision (weeks 20, 30, 40, and the ~100 days after Prelims).** Notes only, three passes: 1st pass all topics, 2nd pass Tier 1 + weak topics, 3rd pass thinkers/models/diagrams/data sheets only. In the ~100-day Mains sprint the optional gets ~25% of the hours (its share of marks), with two full tests in the last three weeks.

**How a working professional fits 400–600 hours across a year.** With ~2 h on weeknights and ~7 h on each weekend day (≈ 24 h/week), the optional should get ~9 h/week in the year before Prelims: two weeknights (e.g., Tuesday and Thursday, 1.5 h each = 3 h, one for reading, one for writing one answer) and one weekend block (Sunday, 6 h: 4 h reading + notes, 2 h one timed question-pair). That is 45 weeks × 9 h ≈ 400 h, which covers a short or medium optional to the end of phase 6. A long optional (Geography, PSIR, History, Law, Mathematics) needs ~12 h/week or a second year; say so to the learner. In the last 10–12 weeks before Prelims the optional slot shrinks to one weekend revision block (notes only, 3 h) and resumes the day after Prelims at ~2 h/night + one full weekend day. Leave for the ~100-day sprint, if at all possible, should be taken in the last 4–6 weeks before Mains, not immediately after Prelims. A full-timer (~55 h/week) can run the same phases at ~15–18 h/week on the optional and finish a long optional in one cycle.

**If Prelims is less than 7 months away: do not start the optional.** Seven months is the minimum in which a working professional can both read GS for Prelims and build mock scores; the optional's 400 hours will come out of mocks and revision and sink the Prelims attempt. Only exception: the optional is your own degree subject and you have verified its UPSC syllabus overlaps with what you studied — then one weekend block a fortnight on PYQ mapping and notes, nothing more, until Prelims. The full optional preparation starts the day after Prelims and then needs the entire ~100-day sprint with the optional at 35–40% of the hours; be honest that this is a weak position and that the next cycle is the real one.

**If the optional was declared and you have changed your mind:** you cannot change it this year once the form and any correction window have closed. Either prepare it (300 hours in the ~100-day sprint is possible for a short syllabus if you are on leave) or treat this attempt as a Prelims-plus-experience attempt and change the optional in next year's form.

## Subject-specific starter maps

Each map: Paper I and II topic lists in UPSC's order (abridged; the syllabus PDF is the authority), the primary source and where in it, the PYQ-heavy topics (2013–2024, from the papers at upsc.gov.in; themes given without a year where the year is uncertain), and a 12-week reading order for a working professional at ~9 h/week (this covers the first reading of Tier 1 topics; the remaining topics follow in weeks 13–22). Chapter titles are given for the widely used current editions; if your edition differs, use the topic name.

### Anthropology

**Paper I.**
1. Meaning, scope, development; relationships with other disciplines; main branches — Ember, Ember & Peregrine *Anthropology*, ch. *What Is Anthropology?*; Nadeem Hasnain's introductory chapter.
2. Human evolution and emergence of Man: organic evolution theories (Lamarckism, Darwinism, Neo-Darwinism, synthetic theory); primates, primate adaptations, living primates and Man; phylogenetic status of fossils (Australopithecines, *Homo erectus* — Java, Peking, Heidelberg; Neanderthal — La Chapelle-aux-Saints classical and Mt Carmel progressive types; Rhodesian man; *Homo sapiens* — Cro-Magnon, Grimaldi, Chancelade) — P. Nath *Physical Anthropology*, chapters on evolution, primates and fossil man; Ember chs. *Primates: Past and Present*, *The First Hominins*, *The Emergence of Homo sapiens*.
3. Prehistory: Palaeolithic, Mesolithic, Neolithic, Chalcolithic, Copper-Bronze, Iron Age (cultural evolution) — Ember chs. on the Upper Palaeolithic and on the origins of food production; coaching notes for tool-typology diagrams.
4. Nature of culture, civilisation, society; culture and civilisation; status and role — Ember ch. *Culture and Culture Change*.
5. Marriage, family, kinship (definitions, types, functions, rules, incest taboo, bride wealth/dowry, descent, residence, kinship terminology) — Ember chs. *Marriage and the Family* and *Marital Residence and Kinship*.
6. Economic organisation (modes of exchange — reciprocity, redistribution, market; formalism/substantivism) — Ember ch. *Economic Systems*.
7. Political organisation and social control (band, tribe, chiefdom, state; law, customary law) — Ember ch. *Political Life*.
8. Religion (animism, animatism, totemism, magic, myth, shaman/priest) — Ember ch. *Religion and Magic*.
9. ### Sociology

2. Sociology as science: science, scientific method, critique; major theoretical strands; positivism and its critique; fact–value–objectivity; non-positivist methodologies — Haralambos, ch. *Methodology* (sections on positivism, interpretivism, values and objectivity); Ritzer, introductory chapters on the history of sociological theory.

4. 6. 7. 9. Systems of kinship: family, household, marriage; types and forms; lineage and descent; patriarchy and sexual division of labour; contemporary trends — Haralambos ch. *Families, households and personal life*; Ember's kinship chapter for terminology.
10. Social change in modern society: sociological theories (evolutionary, cyclical, conflict, functional); development and dependency; agents of change; education and social change; science, technology and social change — Haralambos ch. *Sociological theory* (modernity and postmodernity sections) and ch. *Education*; Ritzer on modernity debates (Giddens, Beck, Bauman).

### Public Administration

### Mathematics

**Paper I.**

## Evaluating optional answers

**What differs from a GS answer.**

- **Depth over breadth.** A GS answer touches six dimensions in 150 words; an optional answer develops two or three with the discipline's own concepts. A GS-style list of generic points is the most common cause of a low score on a 20-mark optional part.
- **Thinkers and schools, by name and position.** Sociology, PSIR, PubAd, Anthropology, Philosophy and Psychology expect each argument attributed: "Weber's 'iron cage' (from *The Protestant Ethic*, in Parsons's translation)", "Riggs's sala model explains…". Two to four named scholars per 20-mark answer; one per 10-mark note. Unattributed assertions are read as opinion.
- **Technical accuracy.** A wrong formula (Mathematics, Economics), a wrong Article or case (Law, PubAd), a wrong fossil attribution or marker (Anthropology), a wrong model attribution (Geography) costs more than a missing point. In Mathematics a solution with the right method and a slipped sign still loses a share of the marks; a correct final answer with no working loses most of them.
- **Diagrams, maps and models.** Geography (landform and model diagrams, India outline maps), Anthropology (kinship charts, tool typology, pedigree charts, fossil skull sketches), Economics (curves), Psychology (experimental designs), PubAd (organisational charts) are expected to draw, label and refer to the figure in text. A diagram is worth a paragraph; an unlabelled one is worth nothing.
- **Disciplinary language.** The answer must read as if written by a graduate of the subject: "function", "structure", "agency", "ideal type", "prismatic", "isostatic adjustment", "Pareto-optimal" used correctly. Newspaper vocabulary in an optional answer signals a GS mind and is marked accordingly.
- **Question-type fidelity.** "Critically examine" needs the critique and a position; "Discuss" needs the mainstream view and at least one alternative; "Comment on the statement" needs the author/school identified and the statement interrogated; a short note needs definition → key idea → one example/critique → one line of relevance, in ~150 words.
- **Indian application and currency.** Paper II of every humanities optional, and the IR half of PSIR, expect one recent Indian example, judgment, scheme, report or event per answer (2nd ARC, a Census figure, FRA 2006, a development from the last two years); Paper I theory answers gain from one contemporary application. Examples dated after 2024 must come from the learner's own reading; verify before use.
- **Length and time discipline.** 10 marks ≈ 150 words in 7 minutes; 15 marks ≈ 250 words in 10–11 minutes; 20 marks ≈ 300 words in 14 minutes. Over-writing one question and leaving a Q5 part blank is the characteristic avoidable loss in a paper.

**Grading checklist (score out of the question's marks; report the band and the reason).** Apply to the learner's text, quoting their sentences when a criterion fails.

1. **Demand of the question identified** (directive word honoured; all parts answered; statement's author/school recognised where there is one). 0 / partial / full.
2. **Core concept defined precisely in the subject's terms** in the first 2–3 lines. 0 / partial / full.
3. **Thinkers, models or theorems used correctly and attributed** (count them; check each attribution; a wrong attribution counts negative). Target: 1 for a note, 2–4 for a long answer; Mathematics: theorem/method stated before use.
4. **Argument developed in depth**, not listed: each main point has a mechanism or evidence, and the answer takes a defensible position where asked. 0 / partial / full.
5. **Critique or alternative view included** where the directive asks (critically / evaluate / comment). 0 / full.
6. **Technical accuracy**: dates, Articles, cases, formulas, data, sequence of steps; no factual error. Each error noted with the correction.
7. **Diagram / map / chart** where the subject expects one: present, labelled, referred to in the text. 0 / partial / full (N/A for subjects that do not use them).
8. **Indian / contemporary application** (Paper II and applied questions): at least one specific, correct, dated example. 0 / full.
9. **Structure and economy**: introduction (definition/context), body in sub-headings or clearly marked paragraphs, a conclusion that answers the question; within the word/time budget for the marks. 0 / partial / full.
10. **Disciplinary register**: reads as the subject, not as a GS answer; key terms used correctly. 0 / partial / full.

**Converting to a score (this chat's bands for a 20-mark part, scaled for 10 and 15; UPSC does not publish its marking scheme):** 14–17 = all criteria full or near-full with 3+ correct thinkers/steps and a diagram where expected (top-tier answer; rare); 10–13 = concept right, thinkers present, one criterion partial (a sound pass); 6–9 = correct but generic, GS-style, thinkers thin or diagram missing; ≤5 = question misread, factual errors, or no disciplinary content. For Mathematics: full marks for a complete correct solution with working; proportional marks per correct, clearly completed stage; little or nothing for a correct answer without method; a wrong method earns nothing even if long.

**Feedback format to use in the chat:** (1) score and band; (2) the two criteria that cost the most marks, each with the learner's own sentence quoted and the fix; (3) the weakest paragraph rewritten to the subject's standard (with the thinker/diagram/formula that was missing); (4) one habit to carry into the next answer — e.g., "open every Paper I note with the definition and the thinker who coined it", "draw the figure before writing a word", "state the theorem before applying it".
