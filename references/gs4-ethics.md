# GS Paper IV — Ethics, Integrity and Aptitude

Scope of this file: Mains GS IV only. Prelims maps, exam structure and eligibility live in `exam-facts.md`; the general Mains answer method and the generic GS rubric live in `answer-writing.md`. Everything below is what a mentor needs to answer "which source, which part, what has UPSC asked, how do I structure it, how would you grade it, and what do I do tonight" for this paper.

## Paper pattern and what scores

**Structure (typical; confirm against the year's paper at upsc.gov.in).**

| Item | What to expect |
|---|---|
| Marks / time | 250 marks, 3 hours. Question paper printed in Hindi and English; answer in the medium declared for Mains. |
| Section A — theory | Typically 6 numbered questions, most split into (a)/(b) parts of 10 marks each (≈150 words); one question is typically a set of three short quotations ("What do each of the following quotations mean to you?"), 10 marks each. Recent papers (roughly 2018 onward) have put Section A at about 130 marks; earlier papers used a 125/125 split with some 15- and 20-mark parts. |
| Section B — case studies | Typically 6 case studies of 20 marks each (≈250 words), about 120 marks in recent papers; 2013–17 papers used a mix of 20- and 25-mark cases totalling 125. Each case is typically a page of narrative followed by 2–4 sub-questions. |
| Word limits | Printed against each question and enforced by the answer-booklet space: ≈150 words for 10 marks, ≈250 words for 20 marks. |

**Time budget.** 180 minutes ÷ 250 marks ≈ 0.7 min per mark. Plan 7 minutes per 10-mark theory part, 14 minutes per 20-mark case, and keep ~10 minutes slack. In practice: Section A ≈ 85–90 min, Section B ≈ 80–85 min. Many candidates write Section B first because a half-written case study loses more than a half-written definition; either order is fine, but decide it before the exam, not in it.

**Why Ethics matters for rank.** (1) Its syllabus is short and its questions are answerable from reasoning plus a small stock of examples, so preparation cost per mark is the lowest of the four GS papers. (2) Marks vary widely between candidates writing similar content, because the examiner rewards *how the thinking is shown*; the gap between a structured and an unstructured script in this paper is often large enough to change the service allotted. (3) Nothing in it goes stale: the examples bank and the case-study template work every year.

**What examiners reward.**
- A one-line definition of every key term in the question before using it (define "integrity" before discussing it).
- One real, named example per point: an officer, an event, an Act, a committee, a Supreme Court case.
- Visible structure: headings, bullets, a stakeholder table in cases, options laid out before the decision.
- Balanced judgment: both sides acknowledged, a clear decision taken, the decision justified on legal, ethical and practical grounds.
- Civil-service voice: "I would…" with a sequence of actions, not "the government should".

**What loses marks.**
- Preaching ("we must all be honest") and moralising adjectives without analysis.
- Vagueness: "take appropriate action", "follow due process" with no named process.
- No stakeholder analysis; no consequences traced; options listed without merits/demerits.
- Only quoting thinkers (a quote is a garnish, not an argument).
- Extreme or impractical choices (resigning in every case; "I would arrest the minister").
- Exceeding length; an illegible, rushed Section B because Section A ate the time.

## Syllabus, decoded, with definitions

Each term: one or two lines, then one example in brackets. These definitions are safe to reproduce verbatim in an answer's first line.

**Ethics and Human Interface**
- *Ethics*: the branch of philosophy and the set of standards that distinguish right from wrong conduct; the reasoned basis of "ought". (A doctor keeping a patient's HIV status confidential even when the family asks.)
- *Essence of ethics*: it is about conduct (not belief), is normative (prescribes), applies to free agents with choice, and is concerned with the good of others. (Honesty is ethical because it is chosen, not forced.)
- *Determinants of ethics*: the sources that shape a person's or society's moral standards: family, religion, culture, law, education, conscience, role models, experience, peer group, media. (A child of a bribe-taking parent normalising "speed money".)
- *Consequences of ethics*: outcomes of ethical/unethical conduct for the individual (trust, peace of mind, reputation), organisation (morale, legitimacy) and society (social capital, rule of law). (Satyam's board failure destroyed shareholder wealth and jobs.)
- *Dimensions of ethics*: meta-ethics (what "good" means), normative ethics (which standards: virtue, deontology, consequentialism), applied ethics (bioethics, environmental, business, media), descriptive ethics (what people actually believe). (Euthanasia is debated under applied ethics; "is goodness objective?" is meta-ethics.)
- *Ethics in private and public relationships*: private = family, friends, where partiality and emotion are legitimate; public = citizens, strangers, where impartiality and accountability are mandatory; the two collide in nepotism and conflicts of interest. (An officer who is a loyal brother at home must still reject his brother's tender.)
- *Human values*: enduring beliefs about what is desirable, guiding choices: truth, non-violence, love, justice, freedom, equality, compassion, responsibility. (Gandhi's insistence on truthful means even when the end was independence.)
- *Lessons from leaders, reformers and administrators*: distilled behaviours, not biographies: Gandhi (means–ends), Ambedkar (constitutional morality), Lincoln (magnanimity), Mandela (reconciliation), Seshan (institutional courage), Sreedharan (punctuality and integrity in public projects). (Shastri's resignation as Railway Minister after the 1956 Ariyalur accident as moral responsibility.)
- *Role of family, society and educational institutions*: family instils through example and early reward; society through norms, religion, media and law; schools through curriculum, teacher example, peer culture and service activities. (NCC/NSS service hours; a school that penalises copying builds a work ethic.)

**Attitude**
- *Attitude*: a learned, relatively stable evaluation of a person, object, idea or event that predisposes behaviour. (A positive attitude to vaccination.)
- *Content*: what the attitude is about (the attitude object) and its valence/intensity. (A strongly negative attitude to inter-caste marriage.)
- *Structure*: the ABC model: Affective (feeling), Behavioural (tendency to act), Cognitive (beliefs). (Fear of migrants + belief that they take jobs + voting for restriction.)
- *Function* (Katz): knowledge (organises the world), utilitarian/adjustive (gains rewards), ego-defensive (protects self-image), value-expressive (shows identity). (Wearing khadi to express a Gandhian identity.)
- *Influence and relation with thought and behaviour*: attitudes and behaviour predict each other imperfectly; LaPiere's 1930s study found expressed prejudice did not match actual behaviour; cognitive dissonance (Festinger) pushes attitudes to match behaviour; the theory of planned behaviour (Ajzen) adds norms and perceived control. (Officials who dislike paperwork yet comply because of monitoring.)
- *Moral attitude*: evaluations grounded in right/wrong: attitude to honesty, to animal cruelty. *Political attitude*: evaluations about power, authority and policy: conservative/liberal, trust in institutions. (Opposing capital punishment on moral grounds vs supporting it as a policy stance.)
- *Social influence*: change in attitude/behaviour caused by others: conformity (Asch), compliance (foot-in-the-door, door-in-the-face), obedience (Milgram), social proof. (Villagers adopting toilets after respected neighbours do.)
- *Persuasion*: a deliberate attempt to change attitudes through communication; depends on source (credibility, attractiveness), message (one- vs two-sided, fear appeal) and audience; Elaboration Likelihood Model (Petty and Cacioppo): central route (argument) vs peripheral route (cues, celebrities). (Pulse Polio's "do boond zindagi ki" campaign fronted by Amitabh Bachchan = peripheral route; Swachh Bharat's "toilet before temple" messaging = central route plus social norm.)

**Aptitude and foundational values for civil service**
- *Aptitude*: innate or acquired capacity to learn and perform a kind of work; for civil service: analytical ability, communication, leadership, emotional stability, interest in public affairs. (An officer who can read a budget and also calm an angry crowd.)
- *Integrity*: consistency between values, words and actions, especially when unobserved; wholeness of character. (Manjunath sealing adulterated petrol pumps knowing the risk.)
- *Impartiality*: acting on merits without favour or bias to any person or group. (A collector allotting relief by damage assessment, not by caste or party.)
- *Non-partisanship*: not aligning with any political party; serving every elected government equally. (Implementing a scheme of a government one personally voted against, with equal energy.)
- *Objectivity*: decisions based on evidence and criteria, not personal feeling or pressure. (Rejecting a contractor on documented quality-test failures, not on reputation.)
- *Dedication to public service*: putting the public's interest and the duty above personal convenience. (Armstrong Pame building a road through crowdfunding when no budget existed.)
- *Empathy*: understanding another's situation and feelings from their perspective. (Sitting with a widow to understand why she cannot produce a death certificate before rejecting her pension.)
- *Tolerance*: accepting beliefs and practices one disagrees with, within the law. (A magistrate permitting a procession of a faith not his own, with proper security.)
- *Compassion*: empathy plus the will to relieve suffering. (S.R. Sankaran's work on bonded labour.)

**Emotional intelligence**
- *Concept*: the ability to perceive, understand, use and manage one's own and others' emotions (Salovey and Mayer, 1990; popularised by Goleman, 1995). Goleman's five components: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skills. (A DM staying calm while abused by a crowd, then addressing their real grievance.)
- *Utility in administration and governance*: better negotiation, conflict resolution, team leadership, stress management, ethical decision-making under pressure, citizen-centric service, reduced burnout. (A police officer using de-escalation instead of lathi charge.)

**Contributions of moral thinkers and philosophers from India and the world**: see the table in the next section.

**Public/civil service values and ethics in public administration**
- *Status and problems*: civil service values (integrity, impartiality, objectivity, dedication, empathy; see the 2nd ARC's proposed Public Service Code) exist in rules, but weak enforcement, politicisation, transfers, discretion, red tape and poor pay-to-temptation ratios undermine them. (Frequent transfers of Ashok Khemka.)
- *Ethical concerns and dilemmas in government and private institutions*: conflicts of interest, confidentiality vs transparency, loyalty vs public interest, efficiency vs equity, means vs ends, personal vs professional ethics. (A doctor at a private hospital pressed to prescribe unnecessary tests.)
- *Laws, rules, regulations and conscience as sources of ethical guidance*: law sets the floor (minimum, external, enforceable); rules operationalise it; conscience is the internal moral sense that fills gaps and judges unjust laws; the working hierarchy is "law first, conscience where law is silent or manifestly unjust, never conscience to override a just law for convenience". (Rosa Parks disobeyed segregation law; an officer should not ignore tender rules because he "feels" a bidder is honest.)
- *Accountability*: the obligation to answer for decisions to a higher authority, the legislature, the courts, auditors and citizens; types: legal, political, administrative, professional, social. (CAG audit; RTI; social audit of MGNREGA.)
- *Ethical governance*: governance that is lawful, transparent, participative, responsive, accountable and just, not merely efficient. (Citizen's charters with redress; e-tendering.)
- *Strengthening ethical and moral values in governance*: codes of ethics/conduct, training (Mission Karmayogi, approved 2020), integrity pacts, protection of whistle-blowers, independent vigilance, reduced discretion, e-governance, leadership by example. (Integrity pacts, recommended by the 2nd ARC and adopted in CVC guidance for public procurement.)
- *Ethical issues in international relations and funding*: sovereignty vs humanitarian intervention, refugees, climate justice (CBDR), vaccine equity, aid conditionality and debt dependence, arms trade, sanctions hurting civilians, foreign funding of NGOs (FCRA). (The TRIPS waiver proposal by India and South Africa at the WTO, 2020.)
- *Corporate governance*: the system of rules and relationships by which companies are directed and controlled: board independence, audit, disclosure, shareholder and stakeholder rights. (Satyam, 2009, shaped the Companies Act 2013 provisions on independent directors, audit committees and auditor rotation.)

**Probity in governance**
- *Probity*: uprightness, honesty and integrity in public office, tested by adherence to procedure and the rejection of private gain. (An officer refusing a "customary" Diwali gift from a contractor.)
- *Concept of public service*: service rendered to the public in the public interest, by public servants held to a higher standard because they exercise delegated public power. (A PIO's duty to disclose even information that embarrasses the department.)
- *Philosophical basis of governance and probity*: Kautilya's king-as-servant, Plato's guardians, Weber's rule-based bureaucracy, Rawls's fairness, Gandhi's trusteeship, the Constitution's Preamble (justice, liberty, equality, fraternity). (Article 14 as the legal face of impartiality.)
- *Information sharing and transparency*: proactive and on-demand disclosure of decisions and reasons; the antidote to arbitrariness. (Section 4 RTI suo motu disclosure; Rajasthan's Jan Soochna Portal, 2019.)
- *Right to Information*: the RTI Act 2005 gives citizens a statutory right to information held by public authorities, normally within 30 days, with exemptions and an appeal chain. (MKSS jan sunwais in Rajasthan that built the demand for it.)
- *Code of ethics*: a statement of values and principles (aspirational, broad). *Code of conduct*: specific do's and don'ts with sanctions (enforceable). (Nolan's seven principles = ethics; CCS (Conduct) Rules 1964 = conduct.)
- *Citizen's charters*: a public document stating services, standards, timelines, grievance redress and the citizen's entitlements. (Introduced in India after the 1997 Conference of Chief Ministers; DARPG's Sevottam model.)
- *Work culture*: shared habits and norms about punctuality, responsibility, teamwork and ownership in an organisation. (DMRC's "reverse clock" countdown culture under Sreedharan.)
- *Quality of service delivery*: timeliness, accessibility, courtesy, accuracy, redress; measured by standards and feedback. (Odisha's "Mo Sarkar" feedback calls, 2019; state Right to Public Services Acts, first in Madhya Pradesh, 2010.)
- *Utilisation of public funds*: funds spent for the sanctioned purpose, economically, efficiently and effectively, with audit (CAG) and outcome budgeting; problems are under-utilisation (lapsed funds), mis-utilisation (diversion) and leakage. (DBT reducing leakage in LPG subsidy.)
- *Challenges of corruption*: abuse of entrusted power for private gain; types: petty, grand, political, collusive, coercive; causes: discretion, monopoly, weak accountability (Klitgaard: C = M + D − A), low wages, social tolerance; effects: inequity, inefficiency, loss of trust. (The 2G spectrum and coal-block allocation cases of the 2010s.)

**Case studies**: a narrated administrative situation with a dilemma, testing whether the candidate can identify stakeholders and values, generate options, decide, and act lawfully and humanely. (See the method section below.)

## Case-study method

**The template (use it for every case, as headings on the answer page).**

1. **Facts and stakeholders (2–3 lines + a small table).** Who is affected, what each wants, what each stands to lose. Stakeholders usually include: me (the officer), the citizen/victim, the colleague/superior, the political executive, the organisation, the public/taxpayer, the law/institutions.
2. **Ethical issues and dilemmas (3–5 bullets).** Name the values in conflict in pairs: integrity vs loyalty; confidentiality vs transparency; efficiency vs equity; compassion vs rule of law; personal safety vs duty; short-term relief vs long-term harm. Name the legal provisions touched (PCA s.7, Conduct Rule 13, RTI s.8).
3. **Options (3–4), each with merits and demerits (one line each).** Always include the "do nothing/comply" option and the "extreme" option so the examiner sees you rejected them for reasons.
4. **Chosen course and justification.** Legal (which rule allows it), ethical (which value it serves, which thinker's test it passes), practical (feasible with the resources and time described).
5. **Action plan.** Immediate (hours), short-term (days to weeks), long-term (systemic fix so the dilemma does not recur).
6. **Conclusion (one line).** Restate the principle that decided the case.

**Time and length.** 20-mark case: 14 minutes, ~250 words (the table counts), never more than 300. Read the case twice (2 min), mark stakeholders and the actual sub-questions, outline for 1 min, write 10 min, re-read 1 min. Answer the sub-questions in the order asked; if a sub-question says "list the options", the options section becomes the body.

**Format.** Headings as above, bullets, a 3-column stakeholder table (stakeholder / interest / concern). Underline the chosen option. Write in first person. Use the names given in the case; if none, give the characters roles ("the SDM").

**Practice schedule.** Working professional, weeknight (2 h): one timed case (14 min) + self-grade with the rubric (10 min) + one theory 10-marker (7 min) + reading from the sources table (the rest). Weekend (7 h): one full Section B (6 cases, 85–90 min) in one sitting, then grading and rewriting the two weakest answers (90 min), then theory reading. Twenty-five written cases before the test series is the target. Full-timer: two timed cases and two 10-markers daily after the two-week reading phase; one full GS IV paper every weekend; 40 written cases before the test series.

**Worked example 1: conflict of interest (district officer).**

*Case.* You are the District Collector. A tender for supplying mid-day meal grain to 900 schools has three bidders. The lowest, technically compliant bid is from a firm owned by your wife's brother. The tender committee, chaired by you, meets tomorrow. The minister's private secretary has telephoned saying the government "wants no delay". A rival bidder has already filed a complaint alleging bias. (a) Identify the ethical issues. (b) What options do you have? (c) What will you do and why?

*Model answer (~250 words).*

**Facts and stakeholders.**

| Stakeholder | Interest | Concern |
|---|---|---|
| 900 schools' children | Timely, quality grain | Delay or substandard supply |
| Me (Collector) | Lawful, defensible award | Appearance of bias; career |
| Brother-in-law's firm | Fair chance to win | Being excluded for a relationship it did not choose |
| Rival bidders | Level field | Favouritism |
| Minister/government | Scheme runs on time | Political embarrassment |

**Ethical issues.** Conflict of interest, actual and apparent (Conduct Rule 4(3): do not deal with a matter in which a family member is interested; refer it to the superior); impartiality and objectivity vs family loyalty; public trust in the process vs the children's interest in speed; political pressure vs non-partisanship.

**Options.**
1. Chair the meeting and award to the lowest bidder: fastest and lawful on paper; but the appearance of bias taints the award and invites litigation.
2. Disqualify the relative's firm: looks "clean"; but punishes a compliant bidder for my relationship, which is arbitrary and itself challengeable.
3. Recuse myself in writing, have the ADM or Divisional Commissioner chair, disclose the relationship on file and to the complainant: slight delay; but removes both actual and apparent conflict.
4. Postpone indefinitely: avoids decision; children suffer, pressure grows.

**Decision: Option 3.** Legally, disclosure and recusal are what Conduct Rule 4(3) and the 2nd ARC's guidance on conflicts of interest require. Ethically, Nolan's objectivity and openness are served without unfairness to any bidder. Practically, a one-day delay is trivial against the cost of a quashed tender.

**Actions.** Immediate: written recusal and disclosure to the Divisional Commissioner; inform the minister's office in writing that the process continues under a different chair. Short-term: committee decides strictly on the evaluation matrix; decision and reasons published on the tender portal; reply to the complainant. Long-term: standing declaration-of-interest format for all committee members; e-tendering with automated scoring.

**Conclusion.** An officer must not only be impartial but be seen to be; recusal protects both the decision and the children it serves.

**Worked example 2: disaster and pressure.**

*Case.* You are the SDM of a hill sub-division. A cloudburst has cut the only road; about 200 pilgrims are stranded at a shrine 20 km away with food for one day; an elderly group of 30 is reported ill. Two helicopters are available for roughly eight sorties today. The Chief Minister's office wants one helicopter reserved for a ministerial aerial survey and media visit this afternoon. The local MLA insists his village, with no reported casualties, be evacuated first. Your junior engineer says the damaged bridge is unsafe, but the contractor says it can carry light vehicles. (a) What are the dilemmas? (b) How would you prioritise? (c) What would you do?

*Model answer (~250 words).*

**Facts and stakeholders.** Stranded pilgrims (life, health), the sick elderly (urgent), MLA's villagers (fear, not danger), CM's office (visibility, coordination), media (information), rescue crews (safety), engineer vs contractor (bridge).

**Dilemmas.** Value of life vs political visibility; equity (most vulnerable first) vs influence (MLA's village); engineer's objective assessment vs contractor's interested opinion; transparency with media vs operational focus; my duty to obey superiors vs the duty under the Disaster Management Act, 2005 to protect life.

**Prioritisation principle.** Triage by risk to life, not by who asks: the 30 sick elderly first, then remaining pilgrims by vulnerability (children, women, elderly), then villages at actual risk. Bentham's greatest good and Gandhi's talisman give the same answer.

**Options.**
1. Comply fully with CM's office and MLA: keeps superiors satisfied; costs one of eight sorties and misallocates relief; indefensible if deaths occur.
2. Refuse all political requests outright: principled but isolates the administration and loses coordination support.
3. Use all sorties for rescue by triage, offer the minister a survey on the return leg of a rescue sortie or by road after the engineer's clearance; send a ground team with food to the MLA's village; keep the bridge closed pending the engineer's written certification.
4. Open the bridge on the contractor's word to speed things: fast; risks a second disaster.

**Decision: Option 3.** Legal: the District Disaster Management Authority's response powers prioritise rescue; the engineer's assessment is the authoritative one on record. Ethical: impartiality and compassion; Weber's ethic of responsibility for consequences. Practical: sorties are the binding constraint; nothing else is.

**Actions.** Immediate: activate the incident response system; medical team on the first sortie; NDRF/SDRF and local porters on foot with rations; written record of the political requests and my reply. Short-term: hourly public bulletin to media and families; helpline. Long-term: pre-positioned rations at the shrine, early-warning SOP, bridge load audit.

**Conclusion.** In a disaster the only legitimate queue is the queue of risk.

**Worked example 3: corruption and whistle-blowing.**

*Case.* You are a junior engineer in the PWD, two years in service, with an ailing mother dependent on you. Your executive engineer asks you to sign a measurement book recording 100% completion of a rural road that, on your visit, is about 60% built with substandard material. The contractor is a relative of a local politician. A senior colleague advises you to "sign and forget, everyone does". You have photographs and GPS-stamped notes. (a) What are the ethical issues? (b) What options do you have? (c) What would you do?

*Model answer (~250 words).*

**Facts and stakeholders.** Me (integrity, livelihood, safety), the executive engineer (wants the bill cleared), the contractor and politician (payment, influence), villagers (a usable road and their taxes), the department (reputation, future audit), the honest colleagues who are silenced.

**Ethical issues.** Integrity vs self-preservation; honesty in public records (a false measurement book is a false claim and abets offences under the PCA); loyalty to a superior vs loyalty to the public; courage of conviction vs the fear a dependent family creates; the normalisation of wrongdoing ("everyone does").

**Options.**
1. Sign: ends pressure today; makes me a party to misappropriation (PCA s.13), destroys my integrity and future defensibility.
2. Refuse silently and seek transfer: keeps my hands clean; the fraud proceeds and villagers get no road.
3. Refuse in writing, record the true measurement with evidence, and report through the Chief Engineer/vigilance and, if blocked, to the CVC under the PIDPI resolution: lawful, protects me by paper trail, may invite harassment.
4. Go to the media or a politician's rival: fast publicity; breaches the Conduct Rules on media, may be seen as motivated, and sacrifices due process.

**Decision: Option 3.** Legal: the Conduct Rules require absolute integrity and allow representation through proper channels; the PIDPI resolution gives identity protection. Ethical: Kant's test (a universalised "sign false records" destroys all public works), Aristotle's courage as the mean between silence and rashness. Practical: evidence is already in hand; refusing to sign a false record is not insubordination.

**Actions.** Immediate: do not sign; submit a measurement note with photographs; request a joint inspection in writing. Short-term: if ordered again, send a reasoned written refusal to the Chief Engineer; complaint to the vigilance wing/CVC with evidence; keep copies off-site. Long-term: third-party quality audit, geo-tagged progress photographs mandatory, e-measurement books.

**Conclusion.** My family needs a son with a job, but a job needs an officer whose signature means something.

## Grading an ethics answer

Apply this to both theory answers (scale the "options" and "stakeholder" rows down) and case studies. Always quote the learner's own lines when naming what cost marks, then rewrite the weakest section.

**Criteria (what to look for)**

| Criterion | Full credit looks like | Typical fault |
|---|---|---|
| Definition precision | Every key term defined in one line at first use; definitions correct and not circular | Term used without definition; "integrity means being honest" |
| Example quality | One real, named, correctly described example per major point; drawn from administration, law or well-known events | Hypotheticals only; wrong facts; the same Gandhi line for everything |
| Structure | Headings, bullets, table where useful; sub-questions answered in order; conclusion in one line | A single paragraph; sub-questions merged or skipped |
| Stakeholder analysis | All affected parties with interest and concern; the weakest stakeholder visible | Only the officer and the superior; the public absent |
| Options and justification | 3–4 options with a merit and demerit each; the chosen option justified on legal, ethical and practical grounds; extreme options rejected with reasons | Decision announced without options; or options with no decision |
| Balance | Both sides credited; no preaching; thinkers used to sharpen, not replace, the argument | Moralising; one-sided; sermon on honesty |
| Practicality | Actions sequenced (immediate/short/long); names the rule, authority or scheme used; feasible with given resources | "Take strict action"; "sensitise everyone"; resignation as the answer |
| Length and time | Within ~150/250 words; written in the time budget; legible | 400 words on a 10-marker; Section B unfinished |

**0–10 scale (per answer; multiply by 2 for a 20-marker's indicative score). This is the mentor's internal scale, not a prediction of UPSC marks.**

| Score | Descriptor |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | Terms defined; every point exemplified with a correct, named example; clear headings; stakeholders tabled; 3–4 options with trade-offs; decision justified on law, ethics and practicality; sequenced actions; balanced; within length. Reads like an officer's file note. Rare. |
| 7–8 | All elements present; one weak example or one missing stakeholder; justification mostly ethical with the legal hook thin; slightly long. The band to aim for in every case. |
| 5–6 | Structure visible but options or stakeholders incomplete; generic examples; decision sensible but under-justified; some preaching; conclusion missing. The average serious attempt. |
| 3–4 | Narrative paragraph; terms undefined; no options, one obvious decision; "the government should…" voice; no actions; over or under length. |
| 1–2 | Restates the case or the quote; moral sermon; factual errors in law or example; sub-questions ignored. |
| 0 | Not attempted, illegible, or irrelevant. |

**Feedback format Claude uses.** (1) Score and band with one sentence why. (2) Three things that cost marks, each quoting the learner's words. (3) The weakest section rewritten in full at the correct length. (4) One habit to fix before the next attempt (for example "define every term in the first line", "add the immediate/short/long split"). (5) Tonight's task: one timed case on the same theme with the fix applied.
