# Mains Answer Writing — Method and Evaluation

Scope: GS I–IV answer writing (the Essay, Ethics case studies and the optional have their own references). This file tells Claude how to teach the method, what UPSC actually asks, how to build speed, and how to grade an attempt. Nothing here is a UPSC-published marking scheme; UPSC does not release one, and it does not publish paper-wise marks distributions, examiner time per answer or band cutoffs. Every score in this file is a *practice score under this rubric*. Where exam practice varies by year, it says "typically" — check the current notification, the printed instructions on the year's question paper and the Question-cum-Answer (QCA) booklet cover at upsc.gov.in.

## What an examiner sees

**The paper.** Each GS paper carries 250 marks and is written in 3 hours. Since 2019 each GS paper has typically had 20 questions: 10 of 10 marks (answer in about 150 words) and 10 of 15 marks (answer in about 250 words). Earlier papers in the 2013–2018 window used other patterns (e.g. 25 questions of 10 marks in 2013; 20 questions of 12.5 marks in 2014–2017), so when working from older PYQs check the marks printed against the question. All questions are compulsory. Answers go into a QCA booklet with fixed space under each question; no supplementary sheets are given, so space itself caps length. Questions are printed in both Hindi and English; the candidate must write in the medium declared in the application form (except where a question asks for a translation or the paper is a language paper). Exact counts, marks and word limits are printed on each year's paper — treat the above as the common pattern, not a guarantee.

**The reading.** Examiners work through large bundles of booklets; assume each answer gets a short reading, not a study. What registers in that reading: whether the first two lines show you understood the question, whether headings/sub-points visible on the page match the parts of the question, whether named things (articles, cases, committees, schemes, data, places) appear, and whether a conclusion exists. Content buried in a 150-word paragraph is content the examiner may not find. Structure and keywords are therefore not decoration; they are how marks get allocated under time pressure.

**Why typical practice answers score low.** They are generic (could have been written without reading the question), one-sided, example-free, or answer a neighbouring question the candidate had prepared. The gap between a low and a good score is closed by directive compliance, named specifics and a visible structure — not by writing more.

**Directive words: what each demands.**

| Directive | What it demands | Shape of body |
|---|---|---|
| Discuss | Examine from multiple angles; present the debate; take a reasoned position | Dimensions (political/economic/social/legal…), both sides, verdict |
| Critically examine | Lay out the claim, test it with evidence, state merits AND limitations, give a judgement | Claim → evidence for → evidence against → judgement |
| Critically analyse | Break into components, show relationships, assess with pros and cons | Components → interlinkages → assessment |
| Analyse | Break down into parts and show how they relate; less judgement than "critically" | Parts and relationships, causes/effects |
| Examine | Look closely into the issue, establish facts and implications | Facts → implications; "critically" adds limitations |
| Elucidate | Make clear with explanation and examples | Explain the statement, then substantiate with examples |
| Comment | Give your informed opinion on the statement, with reasons | Interpret → agree/disagree in part → reasons |
| Evaluate | Judge worth/effectiveness against criteria | Criteria → performance → verdict |
| Justify | Give reasons that support a claim (defend it) | Reasons with evidence; a brief caveat is fine, but do not demolish the claim |
| Substantiate | Prove the statement with evidence/examples | Examples and data that back the statement |
| Illustrate | Explain using examples, diagrams, cases | Example-led explanation, sketch if useful |
| Enumerate / List | Give the items concisely | Numbered points, one line each, no essay |
| Explain / Account for | Give the reasons/mechanism | Cause → mechanism → effect |
| Assess | Weigh significance, extent or success | Criteria, extent achieved, limits |
| Compare / Contrast | Similarities and differences (contrast = differences) | Table or paired points on common criteria |
| Do you agree? | Take a position and defend it; acknowledge the other side | State position in intro, arguments for, concessions, reaffirm |
| Highlight / Bring out | Draw attention to the key features | Prioritised points, most important first |
| To what extent / How far | Measure degree; answer is almost always "partly, because…" | Yes-points, no-points, calibrated verdict |

Two reading rules: (1) a question with "and" or several sentences has several parts — each part earns its own marks, so each gets its own heading; (2) the quoted statement in a question is a claim to be tested, not a fact to be agreed with.

## The method

**Step 1 — Decode (30 seconds).** Underline the directive, the keywords (nouns that must appear in your answer), the parts (count the questions inside the question), and the scope (time period, region, sector, "in India", "recent"). Ask: what is the examiner's expected thesis? Decide your position if the question invites one.

**Step 2 — Outline (60 seconds).** In the margin or on the question itself: intro type, 3–5 body headings mapped to the parts, one example per heading, conclusion type. If a diagram fits, note where. Writing without this outline is the single biggest cause of drift.

**Step 3 — Introduction (2–3 lines, ~20–30 words for 150, ~40 for 250).** Pick one:
- *Definition*: define the key term precisely ("A Citizen's Charter is a public document in which an organisation commits itself to standards of service, timelines and grievance redress…").
- *Data/fact*: one authoritative figure that frames the problem (PLFS, the latest NFHS, Economic Survey, Census 2011, NCRB, NITI Aayog reports — cite the source; if you are not sure of the number, say "around" or give the direction, never a precise invented figure). [Data points must be refreshed from the latest edition of the source before use.]
- *Context*: the event or trend that makes the question current (a judgment, a report, a crisis).
- *Constitutional/legal basis*: the article, act, schedule or convention the topic rests on (Article 21 and the right to life; Part IX, Article 243 onwards, and Panchayats; Section 4 of the RTI Act 2005 on proactive disclosure).
Never restate the question as the intro; never spend more than three lines.

**Step 4 — Body (the marks live here).**
- Headings that mirror the parts of the question, in the order asked. For "critically examine": *Achievements / Limitations / Assessment*. For a two-part question: two headings named after the parts.
- Bullets or short numbered points under each heading; one idea per point; each point = claim + example/data/authority in the same line ("Weak enforcement — charters have no statutory backing; the 2nd ARC's 12th Report, *Citizen Centric Administration*, recommended making them enforceable with remedies for non-compliance").
- Multi-dimensional: run through PESTLE (political, economic, social, technological, legal, environmental) or stakeholders (Centre/states/local bodies/citizens/private sector/judiciary) to generate points; keep the 4–6 strongest.
- Diagram/flowchart/map where it replaces 30 words: a process (how a bill becomes law, how a tropical cyclone forms), a map (regions affected, river basins, mineral belts), a 2x2 (Centre–state × formal–informal), a timeline (phases of a movement). Label it; do not spend more than a minute.
- Counter-view or limitations: in any "discuss/critically/do you agree/evaluate" answer, one heading or 2–3 bullets for the other side. This is what separates a competent answer from a good one.

**Step 5 — Conclusion (2–3 lines).** Pick one:
- *Way forward*: 2–3 concrete, feasible steps, ideally lifted from a committee or an existing model (a state, another country).
- *Balanced verdict*: answer the question in one sentence after weighing both sides.
- *Vision*: tie to a constitutional ideal, an SDG, or a stated national goal (Viksit Bharat 2047, Net Zero by 2070 announced at COP26 in 2021, a specific SDG target) — only if it follows from the body.
- *Authority close*: a committee recommendation or a judgment that settles the point (2nd ARC, Punchhi, Sarkaria; Puttaswamy, S.R. Bommai, Kesavananda Bharati).
Never introduce a new argument in the conclusion; never end on a complaint.

**Time budget.** 180 minutes for 20 questions = 9 minutes average. Target: 10-marker 7 minutes (0.5 decode + 1 outline + 5 write + 0.5 check), 15-marker 11 minutes (0.5 + 1.5 + 8.5 + 0.5). That totals exactly 180 with zero slack, so the real discipline is: never exceed the budget on a question you like, and bank the half-minutes you save on easy 10-markers so that the last 5 minutes exist to fill any blank with a frame answer. Attempt order: do the questions you know best first within each section, but keep each answer inside its allotted space in the QCA.

**Handwriting and presentation.**
- Legible beats beautiful. If your handwriting degrades with speed, slow to the point where every word is readable and cut content instead.
- Underline the keyword in each point, not whole sentences. Headings in slightly larger letters or boxed; no colour pens needed.
- Leave a line between sections; keep a margin for diagrams on the right.
- Use the pen the instructions allow (UPSC has typically specified a black ballpoint pen for Mains; read the admit card and the QCA cover instructions on the day). No overwriting; a single strike-through is fine.
- Fill the space provided, not beyond it: the QCA space for a 150-word answer is typically about one page in normal handwriting; if you are regularly short of space, your intro is too long or your points too wordy.
- Number sub-points; the examiner should be able to count your points.

**When you don't know enough.** Never leave a blank — a blank earns nothing, while a structured frame answer can earn partial credit. Procedure: (1) define the keyword from its parts (even an unfamiliar term can be decoded: "twister" → tornado; "demographic winter" → sustained sub-replacement fertility); (2) write the general principle the question belongs to (federalism, inclusive growth, climate justice, administrative accountability); (3) bring in the nearest related examples you do know (a scheme, a judgment, a region); (4) structure it as if you knew (intro, 3 headings, conclusion). What you must not do: pad with the same point in three phrasings, or write an obviously off-topic prepared answer.

## Building speed and quality

**12-week progression for a beginner** (assumes GS reading is going on in parallel; answers are written on the topic read that day or that week).

| Weeks | Volume | Timing | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | 1 answer/day, 10-marker | Untimed (up to 25 min) | Decode + outline + structure. Use a PYQ from the chapter read. Compare with a model answer after writing. |
| 3–4 | 1 answer/day | 12 min for 10-marker | Intros and conclusions; one example per point. Start the examples bank. |
| 5–6 | 2 answers/day (one 10, one 15) | 10 and 15 min | Multi-dimensionality; counter-view; one diagram per day. |
| 7–8 | 2/day + 5 answers back-to-back on Saturday | 8 and 13 min; Saturday block 55 min | Speed without losing structure. Review Saturday set in one sitting. |
| 9–10 | 3/day + 10 answers on Saturday (90 min) | 7 and 11 min | Half-paper stamina. First full 20-question test on the Sunday of week 10 (3 h). |
| 11–12 | 2/day (keep the habit) + full test every Sunday | Exam time | Attempt all 20; analyse blanks, overruns and weak papers; fix one habit per test. |

A full-timer (~55 h/week) can compress this to 8 weeks by running weeks 5–6 and 7–8 as one block each, and should be writing 3 answers a day plus one full test a week from week 6. After the progression: one full test every 2–3 weeks per GS paper on rotation, 10–14 answers a week from the day's topics, and a growing examples bank. From ~8 weeks before Mains, a full test every weekend and daily answers only from revision topics.

**Daily routine for a working professional (≈2 h weeknights, ≈7 h weekend days, ≈24 h/week).**
- Weeknight (≈2 h): 75–80 min reading the planned topic → 10 min: pick one PYQ or likely question on that topic → 10–12 min: write it under the time limit → 5 min: self-review (below) → 5 min: add any new example/data/judgment to the examples bank. If drained: outline only (5 min) — the frame is most of the learning. Never a zero night.
- Saturday: morning block, 10 answers timed in 90 minutes (mix of 10- and 15-markers from the week's topics and PYQs); 45 min review against model answers; remaining hours for reading. Every 2–3 weeks, Sunday morning = full 20-question test, 3 hours, exam conditions; Sunday afternoon = review and one page of "patterns". Sunday evening stays free.
- Monthly: re-write your three worst answers from the month without looking at the old attempt; compare.

**Self-review in 5 minutes.** Read your answer once as the examiner would, then tick:
1. Did the first two lines show I understood the question (directive + keyword)? Y/N
2. Does each part of the question have a visible heading? Count parts vs headings.
3. Circle every named specific (article, case, committee, scheme, place, figure). Fewer than one per heading = thin.
4. Is there a counter-view/limitation if the directive asked for one?
5. Does the conclusion answer the question or just end?
6. Word count and time: over/under by how much? Which paragraph was the fat?
Write one line at the bottom: "Next time: …" — one fix only.

**Using model answers without copying.** Read the model only after your attempt. Extract three things into your notes: the *structure* it used (headings), the *specifics* it had that you lacked (go into the examples bank, with source), and one *phrasing* worth adopting. Do not memorise model answers; UPSC rarely repeats a question verbatim, and memorised paragraphs show as off-target. Re-write your answer with the borrowed structure and your own words within the same day. Treat coaching model answers as competent, not canonical: they are often over-length and over-referenced for exam conditions.

## Value addition

**Reusable openers** (adapt the bracket; never use the same opener twice in a paper).
- "[Term] refers to … ; in India it draws its basis from [Article/Act/Policy]."
- "According to [source, year], [one figure] — which frames the debate on [issue]."
- "The [judgment/report/event] has renewed attention on [issue]."
- "[Concept] has evolved from [earlier form] to [present form], raising the question of [issue in the question]."
- "The statement reflects [a school of thought / a committee's view]; its validity depends on [the criterion the body will test]."
- For geography: "[Phenomenon] is the process by which … ; it is governed by [factors]."
- For history: "[Period/event] marked the transition from … to … , with consequences for [theme asked]."

**Reusable closers.**
- Way forward: "A three-pronged approach — [institutional], [financial], [participatory] — along the lines of [committee/state model] would …"
- Balanced verdict: "Thus, while [concession], the weight of evidence suggests [position], provided [condition]."
- Vision: "This aligns with [SDG n / the Preamble's ideal of … / DPSP Article nn / Net Zero by 2070]."
- Authority: "As the Supreme Court held in [case], … ; operationalising this is the task ahead."
- Caution: "Reform must be sequenced: [first step] before [second], else [risk]."

**Data — where to take it from and how to use it.** Economic Survey (latest edition, cite the chapter), Union Budget, PLFS (employment; annual and quarterly bulletins), NFHS (health, nutrition, gender; NFHS-5 covers 2019–21 — use the latest round available), Census 2011 (demography, literacy, urbanisation; no later Census had been conducted as of 2024), NCRB *Crime in India* (crime), NSSO/MoSPI (consumption; Household Consumption Expenditure Survey 2022–23), RBI reports (financial), NITI Aayog SDG India Index and National MPI, UNDP Human Development Report (HDI), World Bank/IMF for growth, IPCC assessment reports for climate, India State of Forest Report (forest cover), CAG audit reports (scheme performance). The Global Hunger Index is widely cited but the Government of India has publicly disputed its methodology — use it with that caveat or not at all. Rule: one figure per answer is enough; cite the source; round sensibly; if unsure, give the direction ("has risen since …") rather than a number. **All figures in this file and in the learner's notes are to be refreshed from the latest edition of the source; nothing here is dated after 2024.** Claude must never supply a learner with a precise figure it is not confident of — give the source to check instead.

**Quick diagrams.**
- Flowchart: cause → mechanism → effect (inflation transmission; monsoon onset; how a bill passes; the disaster management cycle).
- Mind map: issue in the centre, 4–6 dimensions as spokes with one example each (use for "discuss" when short of time).
- India map: freehand outline (practise until it takes about 20 seconds); mark regions: monsoon branches, cyclone-prone coasts, mineral belts, river-linking proposals, LWE-affected districts, tribal belts, border disputes, industrial corridors.
- Timeline: phases (1885–1905 Moderates, 1905–1919 Extremists/Swadeshi, 1919–1947 Gandhian; Five-Year Plans; 1991 reforms sequence).
- 2x2 matrix: two variables (formal/informal × urban/rural; high/low risk × high/low impact; Centre/state × policy/implementation).
- Simple table for compare/contrast (3–4 criteria rows, two columns).
Label every diagram and refer to it in the text ("see Fig. 1").

**Do / Don't.**
- Do: name things; one example per point; mirror the question's parts; take a position when asked; keep intro and conclusion short; use the examples bank daily.
- Don't: write a generic essay; invent numbers; cite a judgment you cannot explain in one line; use jargon without defining; exceed the space; use coloured pens, boxes around every line, or three underlines per sentence; moralise ("the government must be sincere"); end with "India will become a superpower".

## Templates by question type

Each skeleton gives the headings and the word budget. Replace brackets. Word counts: 150-word answers ≈ 25 intro / 100 body / 25 conclusion; 250-word answers ≈ 35 / 180 / 35. Where the cited PYQ was set at a different mark value from the skeleton, scale the budget — the headings stay the same.

**1. Policy / institution evaluation (250 words).** *PYQ: GS II 2017 — "The local self-government system in India has not proved to be an effective instrument of governance." Critically examine the statement and give your views to improve the situation.*
- Intro (35): [constitutional basis — 73rd and 74th Amendments 1992, Part IX/IX-A, Article 243 onwards] + [one line on the stated purpose].
- Achievements (60): 3 bullets — [representation: reserved seats for SC/ST and women], [service delivery example], [state model e.g. Kerala's People's Plan Campaign].
- Limitations (70): 3–4 bullets — [funds/functions/functionaries], [parallel bodies and MPLADS/MLALAD schemes], [State Election Commission and State Finance Commission weaknesses], [capacity]. Cite [2nd ARC 6th Report *Local Governance* / Finance Commission observations].
- Way forward (50): [activity mapping and a devolution index], [untied grants to local bodies as recommended by the 15th Finance Commission], [capacity building], [citizen oversight: gram sabha, social audit].
- Conclusion (35): balanced verdict — "partly valid; the defect is design and devolution, not the institution".

**2. "Critically examine" (150 words).** *PYQ: GS II 2018 — Citizen's Charter is an ideal instrument of organisational transparency and accountability, but it has its own limitations. Identify the limitations and suggest measures for greater effectiveness of the Citizen's Charter.*
- Intro (25): definition + origin/basis [UK Citizen's Charter 1991 under John Major; adopted in India after the 1997 Conference of Chief Ministers; 2nd ARC 12th Report *Citizen Centric Administration*].
- Merits (35): 2 bullets with [example: a department whose charter has timelines and a grievance officer].
- Limitations (50): 3 bullets — [non-statutory: the Right of Citizens for Time Bound Delivery of Goods and Services and Redressal of their Grievances Bill 2011 lapsed], [one-size-fits-all, drafted without consultation], [no link to grievance redress; low awareness].
- Measures (25): [DARPG's Sevottam framework], [legal backing as in state Public Service Guarantee Acts, Madhya Pradesh 2010 first], [periodic review with citizen feedback].
- Conclusion (15): judgement in one line.

**3. Comparative (150 or 250 words).** *PYQ: GS I 2023 — What was the difference between Mahatma Gandhi and Rabindranath Tagore in their approach towards education and nationalism? Other recurring themes: parliamentary vs presidential systems; Moderates vs Extremists.*
- Intro (25): define both [A] and [B] in one line each.
- Table or paired points (90–150): criteria rows — [origin/basis], [method], [goal], [outcome/legacy]; 3–5 criteria, one line per cell with an example.
- Convergence (20–40): where they overlap or complement.
- Conclusion (20–30): which fits the question's context, or how both shaped the outcome.

**4. Cause–effect (250 words; scale to 150 for a 10-marker).** *PYQ: GS I 2022 — Why was there a sudden spurt in famines in colonial India since the mid-eighteenth century? Give reasons.*
- Intro (35): [phenomenon + scale + period] with one anchoring fact.
- Causes (100): grouped under 3 headings — [structural: land revenue settlements and tenure], [proximate: crop failure, commercialisation of agriculture], [policy: grain exports, laissez-faire relief]. One example per bullet [Bengal 1770; Orissa 1866; Famine Commission 1880 under Strachey].
- Effects (50): [demographic], [economic], [political — nationalist critique, Dadabhai Naoroji's drain theory].
- Mechanism diagram (optional): flowchart from cause cluster to effect.
- Conclusion (35): synthesis — "famines were policy-induced scarcity, not natural events" + link to the Famine Codes that followed the 1880 Commission.

**5. "Do you agree?" (250 words).** *PYQ: GS III 2021 — Do you agree that the Indian economy has recently experienced a V-shaped recovery? Give reasons in support of your answer.*
- Intro (35): define the term + state your position in one sentence ("largely yes in aggregate output, but uneven across sectors").
- Arguments for (80): 3 bullets, each with [indicator and source — GDP series from MoSPI, GST collections, PMI].
- Arguments against / caveats (70): 3 bullets — [contact-intensive services, MSMEs, employment per PLFS, the "K-shaped" critique].
- Assessment (30): calibrate — which sectors fit which letter.
- Conclusion (35): reaffirm position with the condition under which it holds.

**6. Data interpretation / trend (150 words).** *PYQ: GS III 2015 — The nature of economic growth in India in recent times is often described as "jobless growth". Do you agree? Give arguments. GS III 2024 — What are the causes of persistent high food inflation in India? Comment on the effectiveness of RBI's monetary policy in controlling this type of inflation.*
- Intro (25): state the trend with its source [PLFS / CPI series] and the period.
- What the data shows (40): 2 bullets with direction and magnitude (rounded; verify from the latest release).
- Why (50): 3 causes [supply-side vs demand-side; structural vs cyclical].
- What it means for the question (20): link to the instrument asked about [monetary policy works on demand; food inflation is largely supply-driven, so its reach is limited].
- Conclusion (15): verdict + one policy implication.

**7. Historical significance / assessment (250 words).** *PYQ: GS I 2013 — "In many ways, Lord Dalhousie was the founder of modern India." Elaborate. GS I 2020 — Evaluate the policies of Lord Curzon and their long-term implications on the national movement.*
- Intro (35): who/what/when + the thesis the question asserts.
- Context (40): what preceded; why the actor/event mattered then.
- Contributions / features (90): 4 bullets grouped [administrative], [economic/infrastructural], [social/educational], [political] with specific measures [railways, telegraph, Public Works Department; Doctrine of Lapse; Wood's Despatch 1854 for Dalhousie; Partition of Bengal 1905, Indian Universities Act 1904, Calcutta Corporation Act 1899 for Curzon].
- Critical assessment (50): the other side — [motives were imperial; costs — annexations and the grievances behind 1857; Swadeshi and the radicalisation of nationalism after 1905].
- Conclusion (35): "modern in instruments, colonial in purpose" type verdict; long-run legacy.
Timeline in the margin if dates are many.

**8. Geography process / phenomenon (150 words).** *PYQ: GS I 2018 — Define mantle plume and explain its role in plate tectonics. GS I 2024 — What is a twister? Why are the majority of twisters observed in areas around the Gulf of Mexico?*
- Intro (20): definition with the governing factor.
- Mechanism (60): numbered steps 1→4 [conditions → trigger → development → outcome]; a labelled sketch (cross-section or map) counts as part of this.
- Distribution / Indian or regional context (40): where and why [names of regions on a quick map].
- Significance / impact (20): human or environmental link, one example.
- Conclusion (10): one line — no "way forward" needed unless asked.

**9. Scheme analysis (150 words; scale up for a 15-marker).** *PYQ: GS II 2013 — The Mid Day Meal scheme (origins in the Madras Presidency): critically examine its twin objectives, latest mandates and success. GS II 2020 — "Micro-finance as an anti-poverty vaccine is aimed at asset creation and income security of the rural poor": evaluate the role of SHGs in achieving these objectives and empowering rural women.*
- Intro (20): scheme, ministry, year, objective in one line.
- Design features (30): 2 bullets [who is covered, how delivered, funding pattern].
- Performance (50): 2 bullets of achievement with [evaluation source: CAG, NITI Aayog, Standing Committee, independent study] + 2 bullets of gaps [leakage, exclusion, quality, convergence].
- Reforms (30): [targeting/portability/digitisation/community monitoring], one state model.
- Conclusion (20): verdict against the objective stated in the intro.

**10. International relations — bilateral/multilateral relationship (250 words).** *PYQ: GS II 2017 — China is using its economic relations and positive trade surplus as tools to develop potential military power status in Asia; discuss its impact on India as her neighbour. GS II 2024 — Critically analyse India's evolving diplomatic, economic and strategic relations with the Central Asian Republics, highlighting their increasing significance in regional and global geopolitics.*
- Intro (35): historical/strategic context in one line + the current frame [Connect Central Asia policy 2012; first India–Central Asia Summit, January 2022].
- Dimensions of the relationship (100): headings — [Strategic/security: Chabahar port, INSTC, Afghanistan, SCO], [Economic: trade level (verify latest), energy, TAPI pipeline], [Diplomatic/cultural: summits, diaspora, soft power].
- Challenges (60): [no direct land connectivity — Pakistan/Afghanistan], [China's BRI footprint], [Russia's role], [low trade volume].
- Way forward (30): [specific mechanisms: INSTC operationalisation, Chabahar, development partnership, defence training].
- Conclusion (25): where the relationship sits in India's "extended neighbourhood" and the condition for progress.
A small map with the five Central Asian Republics, Chabahar and the INSTC route adds value.

## Evaluation rubric (used by Claude to grade a learner's answer)

Apply to any GS I–IV theory answer the learner pastes or photographs. Score each criterion, weight, then map to the band. Be an examiner, not a cheerleader: a generic answer with correct facts is a 4–5/10 under this rubric, not a 7. This is a practice score, not a prediction of UPSC marks.

| Criterion | Weight | What earns full credit | What loses it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Addresses the directive and all parts | 25% | Directive obeyed (e.g. "critically" → both sides + verdict); every part of the question answered in proportion; scope respected | Answers a neighbouring question; ignores a part; agrees with a quoted claim without testing it |
| Structure | 20% | Short intro of a recognised type; headings mirroring the parts; one idea per point; conclusion that answers | One block paragraph; headings absent or unrelated to the question; no conclusion or a new argument in it |
| Content accuracy and depth | 25% | Facts correct; points go beyond the obvious; multi-dimensional; mechanism explained, not just named | Factual errors; repetition in new words; vague claims ("many problems", "lack of will") |
| Examples, data, authorities | 15% | At least one named specific per heading: article/case/committee/scheme/figure with source/place/state or country model | None, or wrong attributions, or figures that look invented |
| Presentation and length | 10% | Within ±10% of the word limit; readable; numbered/bulleted; a diagram where it helps | 30% over or under; cramped; no visual breaks |
| Language and clarity | 5% | Plain, precise sentences; terms defined; no filler | Rhetoric, jargon, "sincere efforts", names of cases/acts misspelt |

Scoring: give each criterion a mark out of its weight (e.g. 18/25), sum to a percentage, then convert: 10-marker score = round(percentage × 10), 15-marker = round(percentage × 15). Then sanity-check against the band descriptors — the descriptors win if they disagree with the arithmetic by more than one mark.

**Band descriptors — 10-marker**
- **0–2 (blank/off-target):** blank, or an answer to a different question, or only the question restated with generalities; no structure; no specifics.
- **3–4 (generic):** on topic but could have been written without studying it; one-sided; no named examples or one weak one; block paragraph or headings unrelated to parts; conclusion missing or rhetorical. The most common band in early practice.
- **5–6 (competent):** directive followed, all parts touched; visible intro–body–conclusion; 2–3 named specifics; some depth but points obvious or uneven; counter-view thin; length roughly right.
- **7+ (examiner-pleasing):** every part answered in proportion with its own heading; one specific per point; counter-view and calibrated verdict; an authority (committee/judgment/data with source) used correctly; a diagram or state/international model where it adds; within length; nothing to cut. 8+ is rare and needs an insight the model answer would not have.

**Band descriptors — 15-marker**
- **0–3:** as 0–2 above, or less than half the length with no structure.
- **4–6:** generic; covers one dimension; few or no specifics; structure weak; a 150-word answer stretched.
- **7–9:** all parts answered with headings; 3–4 named specifics; both sides present; conclusion answers; depth uneven or one dimension missing.
- **10+:** comprehensive and multi-dimensional with a clear position; examples/data/authorities on each heading; counter-view integrated; way forward concrete and sourced; diagram/map where relevant; disciplined length.

**Feedback format Claude must follow (every time, in this order):**
1. **Score and band** — "6/10 — competent band (5–6)". For a 15-marker, "/15". One line on which criterion decided the band.
2. **What was done well** — 2–3 lines, specific ("Your limitations section names three distinct failures and cites the 2nd ARC 12th Report").
3. **What cost marks** — 3–5 bullets, each quoting the learner's own words and saying what the examiner expected instead ("'There are many problems' — name them: non-statutory status, no consultation in drafting, no link to grievance redress").
4. **Rewritten version of the weakest section** — the actual text, at the length that section should be, using only facts Claude is confident of; mark any figure to verify.
5. **One habit to fix** — a single sentence the learner can apply to tomorrow's answer ("Write the heading for each part of the question before writing a word of the body").
Then, if the learner is on a plan: name tonight's next question on the same topic.

**Evaluating a photographed handwritten answer.** (1) Transcribe it fully, preserving headings, underlines and diagrams as [Heading], [underlined: …], [Diagram: description]; show the transcription and ask the learner to confirm any word you could not read. (2) Note legibility as the examiner would experience it: words you could not read without effort, cramped lines, overwriting, whether headings stand out at arm's length — this feeds the Presentation criterion and may cap the band if more than a few words are unreadable. (3) Estimate length from lines × average words per line; say "≈170 words" and whether it would fit the typical QCA space. (4) Grade the transcription with the rubric and feedback format above. (5) Add one presentation-specific fix (e.g. "leave one blank line between sections; box the headings").

**Evaluating a full 20-question test.** Ask for the paper (or the question list), the time taken per section if recorded, and the answers (pasted or photographed; allow batches). Then:
- Grade each answer briefly (score + the single costliest fault), and give the total /250 with the band distribution (how many in each band).
- Aggregate patterns across answers: directive misreads, missing parts, no conclusions, same opener reused, examples absent in a particular paper area (e.g. all IR answers lacked specifics), length drift (10-markers over, 15-markers under), diagrams never used.
- Time: where the overrun happened; which questions were left blank or truncated; whether the attempt order helped. A blank costs more than a weak frame answer — count the blanks and estimate what a frame answer would have earned under this rubric.
- Weak areas by syllabus topic, mapped back to the source chapter to revise.
- Three fixes for the next test, ranked, and one for tomorrow's single answer.
- Never report the score as a prediction of UPSC marks; call it "practice score under this rubric".

## Common mistakes and fixes

- **Restating the question as the introduction.** Fix: pick one of the four intro types; write it in under three lines.
- **Ignoring the directive.** "Critically examine" answered as a description. Fix: underline the directive and write the heading for the counter-view before you start.
- **Answering one part of a two-part question.** Fix: count the parts, write one heading per part, split the word budget in proportion to the marks each part implies.
- **The generic answer.** "Lack of awareness, lack of political will, corruption, need for sincere efforts." Fix: no point without a named specific; if you cannot name one, replace the point.
- **Example-free body.** Fix: examples bank, maintained daily, revised weekly; one per heading is the floor.
- **Invented or suspicious data.** Fix: cite the source and the direction; use rounded figures you have actually seen in the latest edition; never a decimal from memory.
- **Over-long intros, missing conclusions.** Fix: fixed budget 25/100/25 (150 words) or 35/180/35 (250 words); conclusion written even if the body is cut.
- **Block paragraphs.** Fix: headings + bullets; the examiner must be able to count your points without reading them.
- **Writing a prepared answer on a neighbouring topic.** Fix: every point must contain a keyword from the question; if it does not, it is not answering it.
- **Running out of time; blanks in the last section.** Fix: strict per-question budget, a watch on the desk, and the 5-minute frame-answer rule for any blank.
- **Over-writing a question you like.** Fix: the 10-marker gets 7 minutes whatever you know; extra knowledge goes into a sharper example, not a longer answer.
- **Judgments and committees cited wrongly or decoratively.** Fix: cite only what you can explain in one line and only to support a point already made; check the report number (Citizen's Charter is the 12th ARC report, not the 4th).
- **Preachy or rhetorical conclusions.** Fix: way forward with 2–3 steps sourced from a committee or a working model, or a one-line calibrated verdict.
- **Never using diagrams.** Fix: practise the India outline and three flowcharts until each takes under a minute; use at least two diagrams per paper.
- **Writing daily but never reviewing.** Fix: the 5-minute self-review with one "next time" line; monthly re-write of the three worst answers.
- **Starting answer writing after finishing the syllabus.** Fix: one answer per night from day one of Mains reading; the syllabus is never "finished".
